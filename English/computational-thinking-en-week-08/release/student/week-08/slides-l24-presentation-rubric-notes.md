## Slide 1 — 2-minute presentation rubric

Frame the supplement. This is the deck the teacher projects between presentations on Lesson 24 — a public rubric so every student knows exactly what passes. Tell the class: "No surprises. Four things, two minutes. Then peer feedback, then the next presenter." Keep the energy calm — most students are nervous before they speak. ~1 min.

## Slide 2 — What to say (4 beats)

Walk the four beats. What the program does — one or two sentences in plain English, like to a friend. How it does it — name the variables, the if/else, the loop, the four parts of the for if there is one. What went wrong — the bug by name, e.g., "I had `scre` instead of `score`." How you fixed it — the one line you changed. Tell the class: "Concrete beats vague. Name the bug, name the line." ~3 min.

## Slide 3 — Timing — 2 minutes

Read the numbers. Roughly 30 seconds per beat. Use a phone timer the room can see. Under-time is fine; over-time eats the next presenter's slot. About 200 spoken words total — that is short. Tell the class: "If you have to cut, cut the *how it does it*. Keep the bug and the fix." Ask the class: "Who has done a 2-minute pitch before?" Most have not. Reassure: it is shorter than it sounds. ~3 min.

## Slide 4 — Pass criteria

The four pass checks. The program runs (or the failed attempt is documented with the error message — honest failure is fine). The student can name in plain English what the program does. One bug-and-fix pair is described. The 2-minute presentation was delivered. Any one missing means not-yet-pass — one more thing to fix, not a failure. Tell the class: "Not-yet-pass is a kindness, not a punishment." ~3 min.

## Slide 5 — What is *not* required

Read the list aloud — every line is a relief. Perfect code is not required; working is enough. Fancy slides are not required; no slides at all is fine. A memorised script is not required; talk in your own words. An elegant variable name is not required; clear is enough. Tell the class: "If you are still polishing the wrong thing, this slide tells you to stop." Ask the class: "Which of these were you worrying about for no reason?" ~3 min.

## Slide 6 — After each presentation

The peer feedback rhythm. One question, asked of the whole room: "Name one thing you noticed." One sentence is plenty. Short, specific, kind. Then the next presenter steps up. Tell the class: "Specific praise is the best feedback. 'I liked how you named the bug' is gold." Ask the class: "What is a kind, specific thing you have noticed in a classmate's work this course?" ~3 min.
