# Week 3 — Teacher materials

This document covers Lessons 7, 8 and 9 — the **describing a process** week. Lesson 7 introduces the simple flowchart and its four shapes; Lesson 8 introduces abstraction as choosing what to leave out for a stated audience; Lesson 9 introduces the informal idea of a reusable, named chunk of process — a *function* without code.

The literal-follower mindset from Week 1 and the decomposition vocabulary from Week 2 are still in force. A flowchart is a picture of a decomposition with branches drawn explicitly; every rectangle has to be small enough for a literal follower. If a box is too big, ask the student for its sub-goal; if they cannot say it in one sentence, the box is two boxes.

Week 3 produces **no AT submission**, but every task this week feeds **AT2 — process description**, submitted at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6. The vocabulary built here (flowchart, decision, abstraction, level of detail, reusable block, function in the Week 3 informal sense) is exactly the vocabulary AT2 will ask for.

Cross-references: the in-class activities used in Lessons 8 and 9 are described in `in-class-tasks.md`. Read that file before Lesson 8. **Activity A — Whiteboard flowchart relay** (mandatory) sits in the 55–80 min slot of Lesson 9; **Activity B — Audience swap** (optional) sits in the 60–80 min slot of Lesson 8.

## Week 3 learning objectives (mapped to Bloom)

Week 3 stays inside **Remembering** and **Understanding**, with light **Applying** where students draw a small flowchart, name a reused block, or rewrite a step at a lower level of detail. Forbidden verbs in Week 3 objectives: *create, build, design, develop, evaluate, judge, analyze, decompose (Week 2's verb), debug, optimise, refactor*.

### Lesson 7 — Describing a process and the simple flowchart

- **Remembering:** the student can **name** the four basic flowchart shapes used in this course — start/end (oval), step (rectangle), decision (diamond), arrow (flow).
- **Understanding:** the student can **recognise** when a process is more than a flat list — when it has decision points or branches.
- **Understanding:** the student can **read** a small flowchart (≤8 shapes) and **describe in plain English** what it does and where each path ends.
- **Understanding:** the student can **explain** why a flowchart is easier to read than a list when a process has branches.
- **Applying (light):** the student can **draw** a small flowchart (≤8 shapes) for a familiar everyday process with at least one decision point.

### Lesson 8 — Abstraction

- **Remembering:** the student can **define** *abstraction* in plain English: hide the detail that does not matter at this level of description.
- **Understanding:** the student can **identify** what has been hidden in a given everyday abstraction (a bus stop, a Login button, a file-naming convention).
- **Understanding:** the student can **explain** that the right level of detail depends on **who is reading the process** and what they will do with it.
- **Understanding:** the student can **recognise** the difference between *hiding* a detail and *forgetting* it.
- **Applying (light):** the student can **rewrite** one step of a small flowchart at a lower level of detail for a stated audience.

### Lesson 9 — Reusable instructions: the idea of a function without code

- **Remembering:** the student can **name** a reused chunk of process with a short verb-phrase.
- **Understanding:** the student can **recognise** that the same small chunk of process can appear inside many larger processes.
- **Understanding:** the student can **explain** what *function* means in this Week 3 informal sense: a named, reusable, plain-English chunk of process with a clear input and a clear output.
- **Understanding:** the student can **describe** the trade-off in one sentence: a named reused block hides detail (abstraction again) but makes the larger process readable.
- **Applying (light):** the student can **draw** a reused chunk as a single named box in a redrawn flowchart, with a short plain-English description below.

## Common misconceptions for Week 3

At least two misconceptions per lesson appear here, each with a `Correction:` line. Pre-empt the L7 one (*"a flowchart is just a list with boxes drawn around it"*) in the first ten minutes of Lesson 7 — it is the most common and the one that hides the *branch* idea from students.

### Lesson 7 misconceptions

**Misconception:** *"A flowchart is just a list with boxes drawn around it."*
**Correction:** A flowchart has **branches**; a list does not. The decision diamond is the shape a list cannot draw. If a student's flowchart has no diamond, they have written a vertical list, not a flowchart. Send them back to find one yes/no question inside their process.

**Misconception:** *"Flowcharts need special software."*
**Correction:** Paper, a pen and the four shapes are enough. The school whiteboard works fine. No drawing-tool homework will ever be set in this course. The hand-drawn artefact is the artefact; the digital version is optional.

### Lesson 8 misconceptions

**Misconception:** *"Abstraction means making it vague."*
**Correction:** Abstraction means **choosing what to leave out for this audience**; it is the opposite of vague. Every box you do draw is precise *at that level of detail*. A vague box is a bad box; an abstract box names a chunk of process and points to where the detail lives.

**Misconception:** *"If I hide a detail, I have lost it."*
**Correction:** Hiding is not forgetting. A hidden detail still exists — the named block has its own description below, the bus dispatcher still has the GPS coordinates, the school's IT handbook still has the click-by-click steps. The detail is filed, not deleted.

### Lesson 9 misconceptions

**Misconception:** *"A function in this week's sense is a piece of code."*
**Correction:** In Week 3 a function is a **named, reusable chunk of process** — described in **plain English** — with a clear input and a clear output. No parentheses, no code syntax, no return values. Code arrives in Week 7. If a student writes a name with parentheses after it, gently swap to a plain verb-phrase like *"log the user in"*.

**Misconception:** *"If I name a chunk, I have hidden everything important."*
**Correction:** A **good name carries the meaning** — *"log the user in"* tells the reader what to expect from that box. A **bad name hides too much** — *"do the thing"* tells the reader nothing. The work is in choosing a name that lets the reader read on without opening the box.

## Lesson 7 — 90-minute plan

**Topic.** Describing a process: list, step-by-step structure, and the simple flowchart.

**Central message for this lesson (one sentence).** A flowchart is the shape a process takes on paper when a flat list of steps stops being enough — and the moment you draw one, you have to decide which steps deserve their own box.

| Time | Activity | Format |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:10 | Warm-up: write a 5-step morning routine on the board as a flat list. Add the line *"if alarm did not go off"* mid-list. The class points out where the list breaks. Read the Week 3 central message aloud and write it at the top of the board. | Whole-class |
| 0:10–0:25 | Instructor input: name the four shapes (oval, rectangle, diamond, arrow). Build the *"should I bring an umbrella?"* flowchart live on the board, shape by shape. Walk both paths in plain English. Add one IT mirror: a two-shape *"can the user log in? → yes / no"* sketch. Everyday before IT. | Lecture |
| 0:25–0:55 | **INDEPENDENT WORK (30 min):** each student draws their first flowchart on paper. Familiar everyday process, at least one decision diamond, maximum 8 shapes, signed and dated. (This is the writing stage of Task 3.1.) Hands and pens, no devices. | Independent |
| 0:55–1:15 | Pair activity: students swap flowcharts and **read each other's literally** without asking the writer. The receiver describes in one sentence what they think the flowchart does and where each path ends. The writer notes the gap. | Pair |
| 1:15–1:25 | Whole-class debrief: pull 3–4 flowcharts onto the document camera. The class names which decision diamond was clearest and which rectangle was too vague. Bridge back to Week 1: *"could a literal follower carry out the rectangle as written?"* | Whole class |
| 1:25–1:30 | Closing line: *"Tomorrow we decide which boxes deserve to be drawn and which can safely be hidden."* One-line preview of Lesson 8. | Whole class |

**Independent-work block: 30 minutes (33% of 90).** Meets the 30–50% rule.

**What you say out loud at the lecture.** "A list works when the steps are in a line. The moment a yes/no question enters the process, the list cannot show what to do in each case. That is when the picture pays off. Four shapes — only four. An oval for start and end. A rectangle for one step. A diamond for one yes/no question, with two arrows out. An arrow for the flow. If you reach for a fifth shape, you do not need it." Walk the umbrella example through start → check forecast → diamond → both paths → leave. Some students will ask for a fifth shape. The answer is *no*.

**Materials.** Board and marker; the *should I bring an umbrella?* flowchart sketched in your notes ahead of time; paper for the independent block. Optional: the supplementary deck `slides-l7-flowchart-walkthrough.md` for the shape-by-shape build, if the live whiteboard build at 10–25 min would land harder with prepared slides.

**Slide cues — main deck `slides.md` + supplementary deck `slides-l7-flowchart-walkthrough.md`.**

- **Warm-up (0:00–0:10):** main deck slides **3** *(The Week 3 central message)* on screen for the verbatim opener, then back to the board for the broken-list moment. No slides during the broken-list build.
- **Lecture (0:10–0:25):** main deck slides **5 → 7**: *When a list is not enough* · *The four shapes* · *A flowchart for "should I bring an umbrella?"*. If the class needs the shape-by-shape build, run **the supplementary deck `slides-l7-flowchart-walkthrough.md` (slides 1–5)** in this slot — one shape revealed per slide. Run the optional supplementary slide 6 (help-desk overlay) only if there is time and the room is still with you.
- **Independent work (0:25–0:55):** leave main deck slide **6** *(The four shapes)* on screen as the silent template. No other slides.
- **Pair activity (0:55–1:15):** no slides; the conversation happens between students with their drawings.
- **Debrief (1:15–1:25):** no slides; the document camera carries the lesson.
- **Closing (1:25–1:30):** no slides; one spoken line previewing L8.

## Lesson 8 — 90-minute plan

**Topic.** Abstraction — choosing what to leave out so the description fits the audience.

**Central message for this lesson (one sentence).** Abstraction is not philosophy; it is choosing what to leave out so the description fits the audience — a bus stop is enough for a passenger, a GPS coordinate is enough for a bus dispatcher, neither is right for the other.

| Time | Activity | Format |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:10 | Warm-up: three pictures or names on the board — a bus stop sign, a *"Log in"* button, the file name `2026-05-20_invoice-001.pdf`. The class calls out one thing hidden behind each. Collect three or four on the board. | Whole-class |
| 0:10–0:25 | Instructor input: define abstraction in plain English (*hide the detail that does not matter at this level of description*). Walk the bus stop with three audiences (passenger, dispatcher, engineer). Then walk the *"Log in"* button: what is hidden, what is not. State the **hiding vs. forgetting** distinction explicitly. Everyday before IT, always. | Lecture |
| 0:25–1:00 | **INDEPENDENT WORK (35 min):** each student takes one flowchart from L7 (their own Task 3.1, or one provided) and **rewrites one rectangle at a lower level of detail** — opens that one box into 2–4 sub-boxes. At the bottom they write one sentence: *"This lower-level version is for [audience]."* (This is the writing stage of Task 3.5.) | Independent |
| 1:00–1:20 | In-class pair activity: **Activity B — "Audience swap"** from `in-class-tasks.md`. Pair A rewrites a shared single-box step for *"a brand-new IT trainee on day one"*; Pair B rewrites the same step for *"a senior IT admin who already knows the convention"*. Pairs swap and read. | Pair |
| 1:20–1:28 | Whole-class debrief: which audiences came up most? Which lower-level versions felt right? Which felt over-engineered? Name one example of each on the board. Bridge back: *hiding vs. forgetting* — when a detail goes into a hidden box, where does it live? | Whole class |
| 1:28–1:30 | One-line preview of Lesson 9: *"Tomorrow we give the hidden chunks names so we can use them again."* | Whole class |

**Independent-work block: 35 minutes (39% of 90).** Meets the 30–50% rule.

**What you say out loud at the lecture.** "Abstraction is not vaguer speech. It is precise speech at a higher level. The bus stop sign says *Park Street*; that is precisely what the passenger needs. The dispatcher does not need *Park Street*; the dispatcher needs the GPS coordinates and the timetable. Same physical thing, two precise descriptions, two audiences. Pick the audience first; the level of detail follows. And: hiding a detail is not forgetting it. Hidden, you know where to find it. Forgotten, it is gone." Resist drift into *"abstraction is a fundamental mode of human thought"*. Keep it concrete: bus stop, Login button, file name.

**Materials.** Board and marker; printed or projected pictures of a bus stop, a Login button, and a file-naming convention example; paper for the independent block. The Activity B pair scenarios need to be on the board or on paper slips before the 60–80 slot. No supplementary deck for L8.

**Slide cues — main deck `slides.md`.**

- **Warm-up (0:00–0:10):** main deck slide **8** *(Abstraction — hide the detail that does not matter)* on screen for the prompt; then board for the three-things-hidden round. Switch off the slide before independent thinking.
- **Lecture (0:10–0:25):** main deck slides **9 → 11**: *A bus stop is an abstraction* · *The "Login" button is an abstraction* · *Level of detail follows the audience*. Linger on slide 11 — the *audience-first* move is the heart of the lesson.
- **Independent work (0:25–1:00):** leave slide **11** on screen as the silent reminder of the audience question. Otherwise no slides.
- **Activity B — Audience swap (1:00–1:20):** no slides; the pair scenarios are on paper slips and the board.
- **Debrief (1:20–1:28):** no slides; the audience names go on the board.
- **Closing (1:28–1:30):** no slides; one spoken line previewing L9.

## Lesson 9 — 90-minute plan

**Topic.** Reusable instructions and the idea of a function without code.

**Central message for this lesson (one sentence).** When the same chunk of process keeps reappearing, give it a name — *"register a new device"* is one named block you can drop into a dozen larger processes without rewriting the steps.

| Time | Activity | Format |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:10 | Warm-up: two short flowcharts on the board, side by side. Both contain *"open the portal → enter username → enter password → press Login"*. The class spots the shared chunk. Underline it on both flowcharts. | Whole-class |
| 0:10–0:25 | Instructor input: give the chunk a name (*"log the user in"*). Define *function* in the Week 3 informal sense — a named, reusable, plain-English chunk of process with a clear input and a clear output. Show *"register a new device"* as one named block reused across three different vocational flowcharts. State the trade-off in one sentence. Everyday before IT. | Lecture |
| 0:25–0:55 | **INDEPENDENT WORK (30 min):** each student finds a reused chunk across their own Week 3 flowcharts (or, if they only have one, between their Task 3.1 flowchart and a sketch of a different familiar process they do) and **draws it as a single named box in a redrawn flowchart**, with a one-sentence plain-English description below. (This is the writing stage of Task 3.4.) | Independent |
| 0:55–1:20 | In-class teacher-led activity: **MANDATORY Activity A — "Whiteboard flowchart relay"** from `in-class-tasks.md`. The class takes turns at the board identifying chunks of a prepared vocational process that could be named, reusable blocks, and proposing verb-phrase names. The teacher redraws live. | Whole class |
| 1:20–1:28 | Whole-class debrief: which verb-phrases worked? Which were too vague (*"do the login thing"*)? Which were too specific (*"open Chrome, type the URL, press Enter"* — that is the chunk, not its name)? Sticky-note collection of one named block each. | Whole class |
| 1:28–1:30 | One-line preview of Week 4: *"Next week — when more than one valid flowchart reaches the same goal, how do you decide which is better?"* | Whole class |

**Independent-work block: 30 minutes (33% of 90).** Meets the 30–50% rule.

**What you say out loud at the lecture.** "When you see the same five rectangles in two flowcharts, give them a name and draw them as one box. The name is a verb-phrase that says **what** the box does — *log the user in*, *register a new device*. The name does not say **how**; that is the chunk, not its name. A named block hides detail; that is the point. The detail still lives somewhere — in a description below the box, in a separate flowchart, in the IT handbook. Filed, not deleted. In Week 7 these named blocks become real JavaScript functions. Today they are a labelled box and a paragraph."

**Materials.** Board and marker; the *"a new student arrives — set them up with a school laptop"* prepared flowchart for Activity A (8–10 boxes, several of which are reusable); sticky notes for the closing collection. No supplementary deck for L9.

**Slide cues — main deck `slides.md`.**

## Facilitation tips

### Before any of the three classes

- **Read `in-class-tasks.md` end-to-end before Lesson 8.** Activity A (the L9 mandatory) needs an 8–10 box prepared flowchart on the board — *"a new student arrives — set them up with a school laptop"* is the suggested scenario. Activity B (L8 optional) needs pair-prompts on slips (audience A: brand-new IT trainee; audience B: senior IT admin).
- **Pre-write your board layout.** For L7, the four shapes. For L8, the three-audience bus stop. For L9, the prepared 8-box flowchart with the chunks you expect students to name.
- **Decide whether to run `slides-l7-flowchart-walkthrough.md`.** Default: yes — the shape-by-shape build is more reliable than a live board build with a tired teacher. Skip only if you know the live build lands.

### During class

- **Lead with the everyday example. Always.** L7 — umbrella before help-desk triage. L8 — bus stop before Login button. L9 — two morning routines that share *"check the weather forecast"* before *"register a new device"*. Every lecture, every time.
- **Insist on the word *literal*.** From Week 1 and 2. If a student reaches for words like *"unintelligent"* to describe the computer, swap to *"Literal. The computer is literal — it does what the box says, no more, no less."* Drawing a picture does not change the rule.
- **Keep abstraction concrete.** If the lecture drifts to *"abstraction is a fundamental mode of human cognition"*, stop and point at the bus stop. Abstraction in Week 3 means a bus stop, a Login button, a file-naming convention. Nothing else.
- **Protect the independent work time.** It is the first thing tired teachers cut. The plans above leave 30–35 minutes; do not trim below 27. L7 is where Task 3.1 gets drawn; L8 where Task 3.5 gets written; L9 where Task 3.4 gets redrawn. Cutting the block cuts the artefact.
- **Use the board for redraws.** Slides set up the question; arrows and named boxes happen on the board. The L9 relay does not work without the board.
- **If a student says "let me write code".** Gently remind: code arrives in Week 7. Today the function is a labelled box and a paragraph. The instinct is right, just five weeks early.

## Answers to common student questions

- *"Do I need an app to draw flowcharts?"* — No. Paper, a pen and the four shapes are enough; the school whiteboard works fine. The hand-drawn artefact is what we keep.
- *"Is a 'function' here the same as in JavaScript?"* — Not yet. This week a function is a **named, reusable, plain-English chunk of process** — a box with a verb-phrase inside and a short description below. In Week 7 the same idea becomes a real JavaScript function. Today, no parentheses.
- *"Why are we hiding details — won't I need them later?"* — Yes — and that is why **hiding is not forgetting**. The named block has its own description below; a real *"register a new device"* lives in the IT handbook. The detail is filed, not deleted.
- *"What if my flowchart has no decision points?"* — Then you have written a list with boxes around it. Go back and find one yes/no question — *"is the milk still okay?"*, *"is rain probability > 50%?"*, *"is the file name following the convention?"* The diamond is the shape that makes a flowchart a flowchart.
- *"Can I draw a flowchart for AT2?"* — Yes — a flowchart is one of the strongest possible AT2 artefacts. AT2 lands at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6 and asks for a process description; the vocabulary you build this week is exactly what AT2 expects. Keep your notes.
- *"Can I use ChatGPT to draw a flowchart?"* — As a sparring partner that challenges your level of detail or your verb-phrase names — yes (see Task 3.5). As an artist that produces a flowchart you submit — no. The hand-drawn artefact in Task 3.1 is the must-do minimum.

## Formative assessment tips

Week 3 has **no summative moment** this week. The checks below are formative — they make understanding visible early so misconceptions can be corrected before AT2 starts. Use one or two per lesson; do not use all of them.

### Per-lesson informal checks

- **Lesson 7 — exit ticket (3 min).** Each student writes one sentence: *"Name the four flowchart shapes and what each means."* If more than a third miss the diamond, recap at the start of L8.
- **Lesson 7 — pair report-back (2 min).** Two pairs name one rectangle in their partner's flowchart that turned out too vague for a literal follower. Listen for the word *literal* in student speech.
- **Lesson 8 — board vote (warm-up).** Three statements: (a) *"Abstraction means making the description vague."* (b) *"Abstraction means choosing what to leave out for this audience."* (c) *"If I hide a detail, I have lost it."* Vote by hands. A strong (a) or (c) vote means the definitions have not landed; re-anchor on the bus stop.
- **Lesson 8 — sticky-note collection (end of independent work).** Each student writes one *"hidden detail / audience"* pair on a sticky note (e.g. *"the Login button hides the OAuth dance / audience: the user"*). Read three aloud at the start of Activity B.
- **Lesson 9 — sticky-note collection (closing).** Each student writes one verb-phrase name for a reused block they spotted in their own week. Read three aloud and ask the class whether the name carries the meaning.
- **Lesson 9 — exit ticket (after the relay).** *"In one sentence, what is the trade-off when you replace four boxes with one named box?"* The expected answer mentions hiding detail and gaining readability.

### What you are listening for across the week

The three phrases that mark genuine understanding by the end of Week 3 are:

- *"The audience for this level of detail is …"* (rather than *"this is the simple version"*).
- *"This box is a reusable block — its name is …"* (rather than *"this box does several things"*).
- *"The detail is hidden, not gone — you can find it in …"* (rather than *"I left that bit out"*).

If those three phrases appear in student speech by the end of Lesson 9, Week 3 has done its job and Week 4 (alternative solutions and evaluation) can build on it without remediation.
