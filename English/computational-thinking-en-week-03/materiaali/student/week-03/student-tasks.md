# Week 3 — Student tasks

## Overview of the week's tasks

This week has **four tasks**. The one with the green dot (🟢 MANDATORY) is the **must-do minimum** — Task 3.1, where you draw your first flowchart from your own life. The three with the purple dot (🟣 DEEPENING) are optional extensions; pick the ones that catch your interest.

**AT2 — process description — is submitted at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6.** The notes you make this week (flowcharts, reusable blocks, abstraction choices) are the raw material AT2 will ask for. Throw nothing away.

The computer is still **literal**. Every step inside every flowchart box has to be small enough for a literal follower to carry out without guessing. Drawing a picture of the process does not change that rule.

## Task 3.1 — Draw your first flowchart 🟢 MANDATORY

**Goal.** Take one familiar everyday process from your own life and draw it as a small flowchart with the four shapes and at least one decision point. This is the **must-do minimum** for Week 3.

### Steps

1. **Pick one familiar everyday process** that you actually do, with at least one **yes/no question** inside it. Good choices: *"should I bring an umbrella today?"*, *"should I take the bus or walk to school?"*, *"which charger fits this device?"*, *"is the milk in the fridge still okay?"*.
2. **Draw the flowchart on paper** (preferred) or on a tablet, using the four shapes — **oval** (start / end), **rectangle** (one step), **diamond** (one yes/no decision, two outgoing arrows), **arrow** (flow).
3. **Keep it small** — maximum **8 shapes** total, Start and End included. If you need more, pick a smaller piece of the process.
4. **Sign and date the drawing.** Write your **initials and today's date** somewhere on the page.
5. **Take a photo** and paste it into your week notebook (or, if your notes are text-only, redraw the flowchart in plain ASCII).
6. **Answer in 3–5 sentences:** What question does your decision diamond ask? What are the two paths out of it? What is the **End** state on each path? (Each path should reach an End oval — no dead-end arrows.)

### Example skeleton (ASCII)

```
( Start ) -> [ Check the weather forecast ]
   -> < Is rain probability > 50% ? >
        Yes -> [ Bring umbrella ]
        No  -> [ Leave umbrella at door ]
   -> ( Leave the house )
```

Six shapes. Yours can be smaller or up to eight.

> 💡 **Tip:** If your flowchart has **no diamond**, you have written a list with boxes around it. Find one yes/no question, even a small one.

### Keep your notes

Keep the dated, signed drawing (or its photo) and your 3–5 sentence answer. **AT2 — process description — is submitted at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6**, and the flowchart vocabulary you build here is exactly what AT2 expects.

## Task 3.3 — What's hidden inside the Login button? 🟣 DEEPENING

**Goal.** Pick one button you actually use and write down what it **hides** from you. Practise the L8 move: seeing the abstraction.

### Steps

1. **Pick one button or menu item** from software you use — the school portal's *"Log in"*, Discord's *"Send"*, Spotify's *"Play"*, your phone's camera shutter, *"Reply"* in your email client.
2. **List at least 5 things hidden inside that button** — things that happen when you press it that you cannot see. For *"Log in"*: checking the password against an encrypted store, getting a session cookie, redirecting you to the right landing page, logging the attempt for the security team, refreshing the menu items you can see. Reasonable guesses are fine.
3. **List at least 2 things that are NOT hidden** — things the button still requires *you* to do. For *"Log in"*: *"you must already have an account"*, *"you must know your password"*, *"you must be on the right login page, not a phishing page"*.
4. **Reflection (3–5 sentences).** Who is the audience of this abstraction — who decided what to hide, and why? Is there anything that *should* be visible that it currently hides?

> 💡 **Tip:** Imagine the button gone and describe what you would have to do instead. That list is exactly what the abstraction is hiding.

### Keep your notes

Keep the button name, the *hidden* list, the *not hidden* list, and the reflection. **AT2 — process description — is submitted at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6**, and naming what an abstraction hides is exactly the vocabulary AT2 expects.

## Task 3.4 — Spot the reused chunk 🟣 DEEPENING

**Goal.** Find a chunk of process that appears in two flowcharts, name it with a short verb-phrase, and redraw one of your own flowcharts with that chunk as a single named box. Practise the L9 move: turning a repeated pattern into a named, reusable block.

### Example pair to start from

Two small vocational flowcharts. Both share a chunk of process — find it.

**Flowchart A — "First day for a new student".**

```
( Start ) -> [ Hand over the school laptop ]
   -> [ Open the portal in the browser ]
   -> [ Enter the username ] -> [ Enter the password ]
   -> [ Press Login and wait for the landing page ]
   -> [ Show the student where the timetable lives ] -> ( End )
```

**Flowchart B — "Help-desk ticket: cannot see grades".**

```
( Start ) -> [ Read the ticket ]
   -> [ Open the portal in the browser ]
   -> [ Enter the help-desk username ] -> [ Enter the help-desk password ]
   -> [ Press Login and wait for the landing page ]
   -> < Are the grades visible now ? >
        Yes -> [ Close the ticket ]
        No  -> [ Ask the user for a screenshot ] -> ( End )
```

### Steps

1. **Find the shared chunk.** Underline the steps that appear in both flowcharts. The shared chunk is *open the portal → enter username → enter password → press Login* — four rectangles, twice.
2. **Propose a short verb-phrase name** for the shared chunk, three words or fewer. Good: *"log into the portal"*. Bad: *"do the login thing"* (carries no meaning).
3. **Pick one of your own flowcharts** (Task 3.1, or any in your notebook) and look for a chunk that might reappear in a *different* familiar process. Propose a short verb-phrase name.
4. **Redraw your flowchart** with the chunk replaced by a single named box carrying the verb-phrase. Below the box, write one or two plain-English sentences: *"This named block does: …"*.
5. **Test the name with a partner.** Show **only the redrawn flowchart** (the named box, not the original detail) to a classmate. Ask them to say in one sentence what they think the named box does. If their guess matches your description, the name is good. If not, the name needs work.

### If you are doing this alone

If you cannot find a classmate, use one of these:

- **24-hour gap.** Put the redraw away. Tomorrow, read the named box fresh. *"Can I tell what this box does from the name alone?"* If not, rename.
- **Class chat or course platform.** Post the redrawn flowchart (without the description) and ask: *"What do you think the named box does?"* One reply is enough.
- **Family member.** Show the redraw to someone who has never seen the original detail. Does the verb-phrase carry the meaning on its own?

> 💡 **Tip:** A good name describes **what** the block does, not **how** it does it. *"Log into the portal"* is good. *"Open browser, type username, type password, press Login"* is the chunk itself, not its name.

### Keep your notes

Keep the example flowcharts, your proposed name, your redraw with the named box, the one-sentence description, and the classmate's reaction. **AT2 — process description — is submitted at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6**, and naming reusable blocks is exactly the vocabulary AT2 expects.

## Task 3.5 — Rewrite a step at a lower level of detail 🟣 DEEPENING

**Goal.** Take one step from your Task 3.1 flowchart and open it up into a mini-flowchart of 3–5 sub-steps. State which **audience** the lower-level version is for. Practise the L8 move: choosing the level of detail on purpose.

### Steps

1. **Pick one step from your Task 3.1 flowchart** that hides a sequence — e.g. *"Check the weather forecast"*, *"Pick the right charger"*.
2. **Rewrite that step as a mini-flowchart of 3–5 sub-steps.** You may or may not need a new diamond. For example *"Check the weather forecast"* becomes:

   ```
   ( Start ) -> [ Open the weather app ]
      -> [ Find today's forecast ]
      -> < Rain probability > 50% ? >
            Yes / No
      -> ( Back to the main flowchart )
   ```

3. **State the audience in one sentence.** *"This lower-level version is for [audience]."* Be specific: *a younger sibling who has never used the weather app*, *a brand-new help-desk agent on day one*, *me six months from now*.
4. **Optional sparring-partner step.** Paste **both versions** into an LLM with the prompt below. **Decide accept / modify / reject** for each suggestion, with one short sentence saying *why*.

```
You are my sparring partner, not my teacher. I am a vocational
ICT student practising abstraction. Below is one step from my
flowchart at two levels of detail, plus the audience I picked.

High-level step: [paste]
Lower-level mini-flowchart: [paste]
Audience: [paste your audience sentence]

Please challenge me. For the audience I named:
- Has the meaning drifted between the two versions?
- Is the level of detail right for this audience?
- Is there a sub-step that itself still needs to be opened up?

Do not give me the "right" answer. Ask me a question that helps
me see the gap myself.
```

**Do not paste the LLM's text into your final flowchart.** The LLM is the sparring partner; the flowchart is yours.

5. **Reflection (3–5 sentences).** Which kind of challenge was most useful — meaning drift, wrong level of detail, or sub-step still too big? What did you keep, what did you reject?

> 💡 **Tip:** If you accept more than 80% of the LLM's suggestions, you are not challenging back hard enough. *"Would I have changed this if a classmate had asked?"* If not, reject.

### Keep your notes

Keep both versions, the audience sentence, the LLM exchange (if you ran it) with your decisions, and the reflection. **AT2 — process description — is submitted at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6**, and the vocabulary you build here is what AT2 expects.
