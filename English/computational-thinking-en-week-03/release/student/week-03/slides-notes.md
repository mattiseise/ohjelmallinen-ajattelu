## Slide 1 — Week 3 — Drawing the process

Open Week 3. Tell students this is the week the course gets visual. Two new ideas land: the flowchart (the first picture of a process) and abstraction (deciding what detail to leave in or out). The literal-follower habit from Week 1 and the decomposition habit from Week 2 both stay. The new skill is communicating a process to someone else — on paper, with four shapes. Ask: "When you explain something complicated to a friend, do you reach for words or for a drawing?" Both work; this week we learn when to pick the drawing. ~2 min.

## Slide 2 — Where Week 2 left off

Refresh the bridge. AT1 should be in. Anyone who has not submitted needs to talk to you after class. The question that opens Week 3: how do you write the process down so someone else can run it? Last week we listed; this week we draw. Concrete: a help-desk hand-off note that a colleague can read on Monday morning and immediately work. Ask: "What was the hardest part of AT1?" Their answer often points to where the flowchart will help. ~2 min.

## Slide 3 — The Week 3 central message

Read the quoted sentence carefully. Two halves: "describe the process so someone else can run it" (the flowchart's job) and "decide which details matter and which can be safely hidden" (abstraction's job). Both halves land this week. Tell students this sentence is what they should be able to say back on Friday. Ask: "If you wrote your decomposition exactly as you remember it, would someone else be able to run it tomorrow?" Be honest — usually no, because too much was in their head. ~3 min.

## Slide 4 — What this week trains

Walk the three lessons quickly: flowchart, abstraction, reusable instructions. The arc: lesson 7 gives you a picture; lesson 8 teaches you what to leave out of it; lesson 9 teaches you to give chunks a name so you can reuse them. Concrete IT example: an onboarding flowchart for a new student account — the second time you draw it, you name "create the account" as a chunk instead of redrawing five boxes. Ask: "Where in your own work do you do the same thing more than once?" That is a candidate for a named block. ~3 min.

## Slide 5 — When a list is not enough

A flat list is fine until the process has to branch. The moment the follower has to make a yes/no choice, the list breaks — it cannot show "if yes do this, if no do that". This is why we move to a picture. Concrete: the morning routine works as a list — alarm, wash, dress, eat, leave. Add the question "is it raining?" and the list fractures. The flowchart handles it cleanly. Ask: "What is one yes/no question in your morning that changes what you do?" ~3 min.

## Slide 6 — The four shapes

The whole vocabulary of a flowchart. Read each one and draw it on the board as you go. **Oval** = start or end (every flowchart has exactly one of each per path). **Rectangle** = one action, one verb. **Diamond** = one yes/no question, two arrows out. **Arrow** = the direction of flow. Four shapes. Make students copy them down. Tell them that on Friday they will draw a flowchart of something from their own life — and they will only need these four shapes. Ask: "Why do we use shapes at all, instead of just numbered steps?" Answer: because a shape carries meaning a number cannot. ~4 min.

## Slide 7 — A flowchart for "should I bring an umbrella?"

This is the first real flowchart. Walk through it shape by shape — and reference the L7 supplement deck if the class wants the slow build. Count the shapes out loud: one Start, three rectangles, one diamond, one End. Eight shapes total. The diamond is the pivot — the moment the list broke. Both branches reach the End oval, which is the rule: every path must finish. Ask: "What if I added 'check if I have an umbrella at all'? Where would that shape go?" ~5 min.

## Slide 8 — IT example — help-desk triage

Same picture vocabulary, vocational context. Notice we have two different End ovals — "ticket closed" and "waiting for the user to reply". Both are valid endings of the process, just different ones. Tell students it is fine for a flowchart to have more than one End oval — every branch just needs to land on one. Concrete: in real help-desk software, the ticket has a status field; the End oval is which status the ticket ends in. Ask: "What is one IT process from your placement that has a yes/no decision in it?" ~4 min.

## Slide 9 — Abstraction — hide the detail that does not matter

Define abstraction in plain English. Land the two negative definitions: not vagueness, not philosophy. The boxes you do draw stay precise — you have just chosen which boxes to draw. The decision is driven by who is reading the flowchart. Concrete: a "Login" button hides a lot of machinery, but for the user the button is the right level of detail. Ask: "Pick one app on your phone. Name something it hides from you." Their answers are abstractions. ~4 min.

## Slide 10 — A bus stop is an abstraction

Three different audiences, three different right answers — same bus stop. The point: there is no one true description of anything. The right description depends on what the reader has to do next. Concrete: a passenger does not need to know the GPS coordinate; the dispatcher does not need to know the timetable. Ask: "If you are the engineer fixing a broken stop, do you care about the timetable?" No — but the passenger does. Both abstractions are correct, for their audience. ~4 min.

## Slide 11 — The "Login" button is an abstraction

Crack open the Login button. The four hidden things — identity server, password check, session cookie, OAuth dance — exist for every login on every site they use. The user never needs to see them. The developer always does. Tell students this is what software design is: deciding which abstraction each kind of reader gets. Concrete: when their browser remembers a password, all four hidden steps still happen — just behind one click. Ask: "When the Login fails, which of the four hidden things might have broken?" ~5 min.

## Slide 12 — Level of detail follows the audience

The rule of the week: same process, same goal, different audience, different drawing. A new help-desk hire needs six boxes before the diamond; the senior admin needs one. Neither version is wrong — they are written for different readers. Concrete: their own AT1 decomposition was at one level of detail; if their grandparent had to follow it, they would need a more detailed version. Ask: "Who are you drawing the flowchart for?" That answer sets the level. ~4 min.

## Slide 13 — Reusable instructions — give the chunk a name

Show the repetition. Five rectangles, twice. Tedious to draw, tedious to read, tedious to update if anything changes. The fix: name the chunk and replace both copies with one labelled box. Concrete IT example: in real software, "log the user in" is exactly this — a named function called from a dozen places. We are not writing code yet; we are practising the habit of naming. Ask: "What is a five-step chunk you have done more than once this week?" ~4 min.

## Slide 14 — A function (Week 3 sense)

Five properties of a function in this course: named, reusable, plain-English description, input, output. No code. Just a labelled box with a paragraph. Tell students this is the lightest possible version of a function — Week 7 will add real syntax, but the idea is the same. The five properties are the rubric: if your named block has all five, you have a function. Ask: "Could 'set the dinner table' be a function?" Yes — it is named, reusable, has input (number of people), has output (table set). ~5 min.

## Slide 15 — Vocational example — "register a new device"

Concrete vocational version. A ten-box flowchart shrinks to seven boxes by naming "register a new device" as one block. The hidden five steps still exist — they live in the school's IT handbook. Land the message: abstraction lets the flowchart fit on one page, and a curious follower can always open the named block to see what is inside. Ask: "If you were a new IT trainee on day one, which version would you want — the ten-box one or the seven-box one with the handbook?" There is no wrong answer; the discussion is the point. ~4 min.

## Slide 16 — Week 3 vocabulary at a glance

Read the terms. Priority terms for the tasks and AT2 later: flowchart, start, end, step, decision, branch, abstraction, level of detail, named block. The "looking ahead" terms (trade-off, condition) are previews for Weeks 4 and 5 — students will see them again. Ask: "Which of these words did you already know before this week?" ~2 min.

## Slide 17 — Must-do minimum vs. deepening

Same two-track structure as Weeks 1 and 2. Read the must-do bullets out loud — they are the floor. The deepening row is depth, not extra credit. Concrete: "spot one reused chunk and name it" is the most useful exercise of the week — it sets up Week 7's real functions. Ask: "Which of these would you actually use at work?" Their answer tells you where to put energy on Thursday. ~3 min.

## Slide 18 — Tasks this week

One mandatory (3.1 — draw your first flowchart). Three deepening. The mandatory task is small on purpose — most of the week's effort goes into the in-class L7 supplement walk-through. The deepening tasks are richer. Remind students that all of this week's notes feed into AT2, which lands at the end of Week 5 or in Week 6. Ask: "Pick one task. What would you draw it about?" ~3 min.

## Slide 19 — What comes next: Week 4 — alternative solutions

Tease the bridge. Week 4 does not add a new computational-thinking primitive — it asks a new question about the work students have already done. "How do you compare two valid solutions and choose?" Same flowcharts they just learned to draw; new question on top. Concrete: two valid backup methods, two valid help-desk replies — both work, but on what basis would you prefer one over the other? Ask: "Have you ever had to choose between two ways of doing something? How did you decide?" ~3 min.

## Slide 20 — Recap — the central message, one more time

Close the loop. Read the quoted sentence again. Pull three or four students for one-sentence summaries. If they cleanly name both halves — describe the process AND choose the level of detail — Week 3 has landed. Remind them about AT2 keeping their notes alive across weeks. Send them off with one beat of silence. ~3 min.
