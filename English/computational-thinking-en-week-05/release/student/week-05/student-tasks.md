# Week 5 — Student tasks

## Overview of the week's tasks

This week has **four tasks**. The green-dot task (🟢 MANDATORY) is the **must-do minimum** — finish it to keep up with the class and to be ready for AT2. The purple-dot tasks (🟣 DEEPENING) are optional extensions.

Keep every note and sketch in one notebook. **AT2 lands at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6.** It will ask you to describe a process with all three building blocks — sequence, condition, repetition — in plain English. The mandatory task is that same artefact at a smaller scale, so throw nothing away.

A reminder before you start: in this course the computer is **literal**, not stupid. Every task is a chance to practise the three building blocks in language a literal follower could really execute.

---

## Task 5.1 — Three moves in one process 🟢 MANDATORY

**Goal:** Describe one process you really do, in plain English, with all three building blocks visible — a sequence backbone, at least one condition, at least one repetition. This is the AT2 feeder artefact.

This is the most important task of the week. The others deepen it; this one *is* it.

### Steps

1. **Pick a process you personally do.** Good choices: doing the laundry; making breakfast; going through weekend photos; closing browser tabs at end of day; checking the family chat; charging your phone before bed. Pick one your hands already know.
2. **Write the sequence backbone — 3 to 6 numbered steps.** One short sentence per step. This is the spine.
3. **Add at least one condition.** Find a moment where the process splits. Write it as `if … then … else …` — e.g. *"If the laundry basket is more than half full, then start a wash; otherwise leave it for tomorrow."* Underline the test.
4. **Add at least one repetition.** Find a moment where the same step happens more than once. Write it as `for each …`, `repeat until …`, or `while …` — e.g. *"For each photo in the trip folder, decide: keep or delete."* Underline the stopping condition.
5. **Draw the condition as a small decision tree** on paper or in a notes app. Rough sketch is fine.
6. **Re-read as a literal follower.** Pretend a colleague handed it to you with no extra explanation. Mark any word you would have to ask about.
7. **Write a 3–5 sentence reflection.** Which building block was hardest to name? Where did sequence and repetition blur? Was your decision-tree test honest, or did you write something like *"if it goes wrong"* that a literal follower could not check?

### If you are doing this alone

This task does not assume a partner. The "re-read as a literal follower" step works much better with a 24-hour gap — **write your description today, come back tomorrow** with no memory of what you meant, and read it as if a stranger handed it to you. If you can, also walk through it on a **different device or in a different room** — the change of context surfaces hidden assumptions.

> 💡 **Tip:** Resist the urge to write code-looking syntax. Plain English with `if … then … else …` and `for each …` is exactly what the course wants. Pseudocode arrives next week.

### Keep your notes

Keep your sequence, your condition rewrite, your repetition with its stopping condition, your decision-tree sketch and your reflection. **AT2 lands at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6 and will ask you to scale exactly this artefact up.** The work you do here is half the work of AT2.

## Task 5.2 — Decision tree for a real rule 🟣 DEEPENING

**Goal:** Practise drawing a decision tree for a rule you did not invent — a rule that already governs some part of your life. Train your eye to spot the test on each branch.

### Steps

1. **Pick a real rule with at least two branches.** Suggestions: your school's lateness policy; the family rule about screens after dinner; a website's account-recovery flow; a job application's eligibility checklist.
2. **Write the rule in one or two plain-English sentences.** Copy from the source if you have it; paraphrase if not.
3. **Draw the decision tree.** At the top, the **root question** — the first test. Branches for each possible answer (usually two or three). At the end of each branch, what happens or the next question.
4. **Name the test on every branch honestly.** *"If it goes wrong"* is not a test a literal follower can run. *"If the time is later than 8:15"* is.
5. **Write 3–5 sentences** about which branch was hardest to test honestly, and why.
6. **Optional — compound conditions.** If your rule contains an AND or OR (*"if you are late AND you have no excuse, sign the late book"*), rewrite it as two simpler tests in a row and note which form felt clearer.

### If you are doing this alone

This task does not assume a partner. The 24-hour gap helps: come back the next day and redraw the tree from memory before looking at your first version. Branches you forget are branches a literal follower might miss too.

> 💡 **Tip:** A tree with three or four branches is normal. More than five usually means the rule has a hidden inner question on one of the branches.

### Keep your notes

Keep the rule, the tree and your reflection. **AT2 lands at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6** and will ask you to put a real decision tree behind at least one condition in your process description.

## Task 5.3 — The stopping condition is the safety belt 🟣 DEEPENING

**Goal:** Train yourself to spot the moment a repetition could run forever, and to add an honest, observable stop.

### Steps

1. **Write three short repetitions** using `for each …`, `repeat until …`, or `while …`. For example:
   - *"Keep refreshing the page until the seat is available."*
   - *"For each photo from the weekend, decide if it should be deleted."*
   - *"While the train has not arrived, keep checking the platform sign."*
   Write your own three; do not just copy these.
2. **For each, ask: what if the stop never happens?** Write one sentence describing the **runaway case**.
3. **Add a safety stop.** Rewrite each repetition with a second stopping condition. Examples: *"…or stop after 10 tries"*; *"…or stop after 30 minutes"*; *"…or leave the platform if the train is more than 20 minutes late"*.
4. **Reflect in 3–5 sentences.** Was any original stop actually impossible to observe? Did adding a safety stop change the meaning of the repetition, or just make it honest?

### If you are doing this alone

This task does not assume a partner. If you want a second opinion, read your three out to a classmate or family member and ask: *"If you were the literal follower of this, when exactly would you stop?"* Any hesitation in their answer is a hint that your stop is not observable.

> 💡 **Tip:** A safety stop is not a failure of design. Real systems use them everywhere — login screens lock accounts after a few failed tries, downloads time out, kettles switch off when water boils.

### Keep your notes

Keep your three repetitions, the runaway-case sentences and the rewritten safety-stop versions. **AT2 lands at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6** and will check whether every repetition in your process description has an honest stop.

## Task 5.4 — Vocational angle: password rule, ticket routing, or inbox sweep 🟣 DEEPENING

**Goal:** Apply the three building blocks to a realistic IT scene. Pick one of three classic vocational examples and describe it in plain English.

### Steps

1. **Pick one of the three scenes:**
   - **(a) Password validation rule.** *"If the password is shorter than 8 characters, reject it. If it has no number, reject it. If it has no capital letter, reject it. Otherwise accept it."* — heavy on conditions.
   - **(b) Help-desk ticket routing.** *"If the ticket mentions 'login' or 'password', send it to Identity. If it mentions a printer, send it to Hardware. Otherwise send it to General Support."*
   - **(c) Inbox sweep.** *"For each unread message: if it is from a person, mark it for reply; if it is a newsletter, archive it; if it is spam, delete it. Repeat until the inbox is empty."* — all three building blocks visible.
2. **Write the rule or process in plain English**, using all three building blocks where they fit.
3. **Draw the decision tree** for the conditions in your description. (Even an inbox sweep has a small tree inside it — *person / newsletter / spam*.)
4. **Write a 3–5 sentence reflection.** Which building block dominated your rule? Was there a default *otherwise* branch — what does the follower do if no test matches?

### Optional LLM sparring step

Once your description and tree are written, you can paste your work into an LLM. **Do not accept the LLM's output verbatim.** Treat it as a sparring partner — for every suggestion, decide whether to keep, modify or reject it, and write one sentence saying why.

```
You are my sparring partner, not my teacher. I am a vocational ICT
student practising sequence, condition and repetition in plain
English.

Below is my description of a [password rule / ticket routing rule /
inbox sweep] using "if … then … else …" and "for each …" /
"repeat until …" in natural language:

[paste your description here]

Please challenge me:
- Is any condition I wrote ambiguous? Name the ambiguous word.
- Is any repetition missing an honest stopping condition?
- Did I confuse a one-off step (sequence) with a repeated step?
Do not give me the "right" answer outright. Ask me a question
that helps me see the gap myself.
```

### If you are doing this alone

This task does not assume a human partner — the LLM step is optional. If you would like a human reader, post your description in the class chat: *"Would anyone read this and tell me if any condition is unclear?"* The 24-hour gap technique also works here.

> 💡 **Tip:** Vocational rules in the wild often hide an *otherwise* branch that the original writer left out. Ask: *what does a literal follower do if no test in my rule matches?*

### Keep your notes

Keep your rule, your tree, your reflection, and (if you used the LLM) your decide/modify/reject notes. **AT2 lands at the end of Week 5 / during Week 6** and may invite you to use a vocational process as your AT2 subject.
