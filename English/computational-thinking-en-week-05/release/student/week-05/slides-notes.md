## Slide 1 — Week 5 — Three moves: sequence, condition, repetition

Open with a hook. Tell the class that everything they have done so far — the literal-robot exercises, the noodles recipe, the simple checklists — has been quietly preparing them for one big idea. By the end of this week they will look at any process and see three moves. Ask the class: "Think of one thing you did this morning that had steps. Hold onto it — we will name its parts before the bell." Keep it light, no code yet. ~2 min.

## Slide 2 — The central message

Read the bold sentence aloud, slowly. Say: "Sequence, condition, repetition — those are the three. If you can name them in a tea recipe, you can name them in a program." This matters because students often think programming is mysterious; it is not — it is three patterns rearranged. Ask the class: "Has anyone heard these words before? Where?" Keep it under a minute. ~1 min.

## Slide 3 — What this week trains

Walk through the three lessons in one breath. Make the promise explicit: by Friday they will be able to point at any process and say "that bit is a condition, that bit is a loop." Emphasize: still no code. The point of Week 5 is the eyes, not the keyboard. Ask the class: "Which of the three words sounds easiest? Which sounds hardest?" Quick show of hands, no debate. ~2 min.

## Slide 4 — Sequence — the default move

This is the easiest of the three — doing one thing after another. The tea example is on purpose: every student has done it, no IT vocabulary in the way. Read the four steps. Point out that sequence is so obvious we forget it counts. But computers cannot guess. Ask the class: "What happens if you skip step 1?" Concrete: an empty kettle clicks immediately. ~2 min.

## Slide 5 — When order matters

Read the broken version aloud, with theatre. The "literal follower" image is the same trick as Week 1's literal robot. Stress: order is part of meaning, not decoration. Ask the class: "Which IT task you know would also break if you reordered two steps?" Possible answers: install before plug in, save before close, format before partition. ~2 min.

## Slide 6 — Sequence at work — deployment checklist

Now the same shape in a realistic vocational scene. Read it as a real deploy day. Emphasize that "merge before build before test before deploy" is not a style choice — it is forced by physics. The deployment checklist is the tea recipe in a hoodie. Ask the class: "Which two steps absolutely cannot swap?" Steer them to merge before build, or sign-off before production. ~2 min.

## Slide 7 — Condition — a test that splits the path

Pivot to the second move. A condition is the moment the road forks. Read the umbrella example, then stress the word *test* — it has to be something checkable, not a feeling. This matters because vague tests produce vague code later. Ask the class: "Name one condition you checked already today." Examples: alarm not yet, train on time, phone charged. ~2 min.

## Slide 8 — if … then … else …

Two sentences, same shape. Point at "if … then … else" in the air. Tell them this exact phrase will appear in pseudocode next week and in real code the week after — they are already learning the keyword, just without the brackets. Ask the class: "Translate the IT mirror into the umbrella style — what is the 'rain'?" Answer: the short password. ~2 min.

## Slide 9 — Decision tree — drawing the split

Draw the tree on the board even though the slide has it. Two branches, two leaves. Stress: pen and paper is enough. The point is honest branches. If a branch is fuzzy, the program will be too. Ask the class: "Could 'no' also lead to another question?" Yes — trees can grow. We will deepen this in the L14 supplement. ~2 min.

## Slide 10 — Condition at work — password validation

Read each rule. Note that the chain of conditions ends in only two real outcomes: accept or reject. The decision tree could draw this with three "rejects" leaves and one "accept" leaf. Ask the class: "Which rule would you add for a stronger password?" Common answers: special character, no name, no dictionary word. Avoid debating policy — focus on the *shape*. ~2 min.

## Slide 11 — Repetition — doing it again, with a stop

Third move. The keyword phrase is "with a stop" — say it twice. A repetition without a stop is the bug that crashes a browser, drains a battery, locks a server. The register example is everyday and observable: the stop is "we ran out of names." Ask the class: "Where in your phone do you see repetition every day?" Examples: scrolling a feed, notification check loop. ~2 min.

## Slide 12 — Three shapes of repetition

Three flavors, same idea. *For each* is when you know the list (the register). *Repeat until* is when you stop on an event. *While* is when you check before each turn. Do not over-formalize — they will meet these as keywords in Week 6. Ask the class: "Brushing teeth — is that for-each, repeat-until, or while?" Trick question — depends on the stop ("two minutes" vs. "feels clean"). ~2 min.

## Slide 13 — Repetition at work — the inbox sweep

Read the block slowly. Point out: a *for each* repetition with an *if/else if/else if* condition chain inside, ending with a *repeat until* style stop. All three moves in five lines. This is the shape of real workflow code. Ask the class: "Which line is the condition? Which is the loop? Which is the sequence?" Let two students answer. ~2 min.

## Slide 14 — The stopping condition is the safety belt

This slide protects them from a real bug. An honest stop is something the follower can *see*. "Until you feel done" is not a stop a program can check. Compare to a kitchen timer vs. "until it tastes right." Ask the class: "Rewrite 'until you understand' as an honest stop for a quiz app." Possible: until 8 out of 10 correct in a row. ~2 min.

## Slide 15 — Three moves in one process

Now the synthesis. Most real processes are a weave: a small sequence inside a condition inside a loop. Naming the three is what makes the description readable for the next reader (or future you). Ask the class: "Pick any app you used this morning. Name its three moves." Examples: Spotify autoplay loop, message app notification check, transit app route choice. ~2 min.

## Slide 16 — Must-do minimum vs. deepening

Be explicit about the bar. The minimum: name the three moves in any process, then write one short description that combines all three. That is enough. Deepening is for students who finished and want more — compound conditions, runaway loops, vocational scenes, LLM sparring. Ask the class: "Which deepening task sounds most useful for your field?" ~2 min.

## Slide 17 — Tasks this week

Walk through the four tasks. Task 5.1 is mandatory and feeds AT2. The deepening tasks are optional but earn the higher AT2 grade. Stress: keep your notes. The pseudocode work in Week 6 reuses these. Ask the class: "Pick one mandatory and one deepening — which two?" Have them write the pair in their notebook. ~2 min.

## Slide 18 — What comes next: Week 6 — pseudocode

Preview Week 6 without overpromising. The English they write this week gets light conventions next week: small variables, a tighter shape, a trace table. The thinking does not change. The shape just tightens. Ask the class: "Does pseudocode sound scarier or easier than 'code'?" Most will say easier — that is the point. ~1 min.

## Slide 19 — Recap — the central message, one more time

End by repeating the opening message word for word. Sequence, condition, repetition — three moves. If you can name them in a tea recipe, you can name them in a program. Ask the class one final time: "What were the three moves?" Wait for the chorus. End on time. ~1 min.
