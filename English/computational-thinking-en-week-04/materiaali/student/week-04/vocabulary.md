# Vocabulary

## Week 4 vocabulary

The terms below cover Lessons 10, 11 and 12. Definitions are short on purpose — the self-study has each term in context with examples. *Criterion / criteria*, the four named criteria, and *trade-off* are the new heart of the week. One term at the end (*iteration*) is a looking-ahead preview of Week 5 and AT2/AT3, flagged as such.

### Valid solution

A description of a process that actually reaches the goal of the problem it claims to solve. A morning routine that gets you out the door on time is a valid solution to *"get to school"*. A backup procedure that produces a recoverable copy of the folder is a valid solution to *"back up the project"*. Validity is a yes/no property — either the solution reaches the goal or it does not. It does not yet say *how well* the solution does it; that is what the criteria are for.

### Alternative (solution)

A second valid solution to the same problem, different in steps, order, or in what it leaves to the user. Two tea recipes that both produce a drinkable cup of tea are alternatives. Two backup procedures that both produce a recoverable copy of the folder are alternatives. *Alternative* is the word for *"valid, but not the same as the other one"*.

### Criterion (singular)

A stated yardstick you use to compare solutions on one named property. *Clarity* is a criterion. *Efficiency* is another. A criterion is the thing that turns the question *"which is better?"* from a matter of taste into a question with a defensible answer. The plural is *criteria*.

### Criteria (plural)

More than one criterion. The four criteria for this course are *clarity*, *efficiency*, *repeatability* and *error-proneness*, in that fixed order on first mention. When you compare two solutions, you go through the criteria one at a time — never all at once and never as an average score.

### Clarity (as a criterion)

How easy a solution is to read once and act on without asking questions. A clear instruction names the right step, the right object and the right next move; an unclear one leaves the literal follower guessing. Everyday: *"add a tea bag"* is clearer than *"add the thing"*. IT: *"save the file as `2026-05-20_invoice.pdf` on the shared drive"* is clearer than *"save it where it should go"*.

### Efficiency (as a criterion)

How free the solution is of wasted steps. An efficient solution reaches the goal without detours, duplicates or rework — *no wasted steps*, not just *fast*. Everyday: heating the water once is more efficient than boiling, cooling, and reboiling. IT: zipping a folder once and uploading the zip is more efficient than copying twenty separate files one by one.

### Repeatability (as a criterion)

How reliably the solution produces the same result the second, third and tenth time, with the same input. A repeatable solution does not depend on the follower's mood, memory, or guesswork. Everyday: a tea recipe that always uses 200 ml of water and one bag is more repeatable than *"a bit of water, taste it, add another bag if it's weak"*. IT: a backup that runs from a saved script is more repeatable than a backup that depends on remembering where the folder lives this month.

### Error-proneness (as a criterion)

How easy it is to do the wrong thing while following the solution. Low error-proneness means few easy mistakes, few foot-guns; high error-proneness means the next person down the line is one slip away from the wrong outcome. Everyday: a recipe that says *"pour the boiling water on the bag, then sit it for three minutes"* is less error-prone than *"pour the water on, count to a bit"*. IT: a backup that names the date in the file is less error-prone than a backup that overwrites the same `backup.zip` every time. (Some textbooks call this *robustness*; in this course we keep *error-proneness*.)

### Trade-off

A situation where two criteria pull in different directions, so improving one means giving up some of the other. The clearer recipe may need more steps and so look less efficient on paper; the faster backup may be more error-prone. A trade-off is not a problem to solve — it is the *shape* of the comparison. Naming the trade-off is the analysis.

### Peer review

A short, structured cycle where a partner reads your solution and writes specific, criterion-named comments on it, and you do the same for theirs. Peer review in this course is not a grade — it is a way to read your own work *with someone else's eyes*. The protocol for Week 4 is **criteria → feedback → revision**.

### Feedback (criterion-named)

A comment that names a specific criterion and points to a specific step or block. *"This is unclear"* is taste in disguise. *"This is unclear at step 3 because the word 'file' doesn't say which file"* is feedback you can act on. The Week 4 rule of thumb: every piece of feedback names a criterion and a concrete spot.

### Revision

A reworked version of one of your own existing artefacts (a Week 2 decomposition or a Week 3 flowchart / algorithm) made in response to feedback. A revision is **not a new creation** — you are not starting from scratch. You change one or two specific things in the original to address one or two specific pieces of criterion-named feedback, and you write a 3–5 sentence change note saying what changed and which criterion drove it.

### Rubric (informal)

An informal grid of criteria you use to look at a solution one criterion at a time — for this course, the four criteria in a four-row table. The Week 4 rubric is **descriptive**, not numerical: each cell holds one short sentence, never a number, a score, or a grade. *"Recipe A is clearer at step 3 because…"* is rubric output. *"Recipe A scores 4/5 on clarity"* is not.

### Reviewer

The person currently reading someone else's artefact and writing criterion-named feedback on it. The reviewer's job is to **describe**, not **judge**: which criterion does this step speak to, and why. In the L12 protocol the reviewer does a silent read first, then writes one sentence per criterion, then picks the two most useful comments to hand over.

### Author (of an artefact)

The person who wrote the original artefact under review. The author's job during peer review is to **receive** the feedback, ask only **clarifying** questions (no defending), and then choose which piece of feedback to act on in the revision. The author may reasonably reject feedback — they just have to be able to say which criterion it speaks to and why it does not apply here.

### Iteration *(looking ahead — central in Week 5 and reused in AT2/AT3)*

The idea of going around the same loop more than once — *do, look, change, do again*. Week 4 trains a single revision pass: read, feedback, revise. Week 5 turns the same idea into a formal logic structure (a loop with a condition). AT2 in Week 5/6 asks for a process description plus one revision after feedback; AT3 in Week 8 asks for the same habit on a small JavaScript program. The peer-review-and-revision cycle you practise this week is the first version of *iteration*.
