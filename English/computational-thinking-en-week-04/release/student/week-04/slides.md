## A problem is not the same as its solution

The **problem** is the goal: *"make a drinkable cup of tea"*, *"have a recoverable backup of the project folder"*.

The **solution** is one way of reaching that goal — the steps you write down.

One problem. Many possible solutions, all valid.

---

## Two ways to make tea

| Step | Recipe A (boil first) | Recipe B (steep first) |
|------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| 1 | Fill the kettle with 250 ml of water | Put one tea bag into the cup |
| 2 | Boil the water | Fill the kettle with 250 ml of water |
| 3 | Put one tea bag into the cup | Boil the water |
| 4 | Pour the boiled water over the bag | Pour the boiled water over the bag |
| 5 | Wait 3 minutes, remove the bag | Wait until the colour looks right, remove the bag |

Both produce a drinkable cup of tea. They differ in *order* and in what is left to the user.

---

## Two ways to back up a project folder

| Step | Method A (USB copy) | Method B (zip + email) |
|------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| 1 | Plug the USB drive into the laptop | Right-click the project folder |
| 2 | Open the project folder | Choose *"Compress to .zip"* |
| 3 | Select all files, press Ctrl+C | Wait for the zip file to appear |
| 4 | Open the USB drive | Open your school email |
| 5 | Press Ctrl+V, wait for copy to finish | Attach the zip, send it to yourself |
| 6 | Eject the USB drive safely | Save the email to a *Backups* folder |

Both produce a recoverable backup. They differ in *where the copy lives* and in *what can go wrong*.

---

## What is the same, what is different?

- **Same** — both reach the goal, both are valid.
- **Different** — the *step order* changes (boil-first vs. steep-first).
- **Different** — what is *left to the user* (count three minutes vs. judge the colour).
- **Different** — what can *go wrong* (forgetting the bag vs. forgetting to eject the drive).

That last list is the start of the criteria.

## From taste to criterion

*"I just like Recipe A better."* — taste.

*"Recipe A is clearer at step 5 because *3 minutes* tells the follower exactly when to stop."* — criterion-named.

> **More than one valid solution can solve the same problem. Picking between them is not a matter of taste — it is a matter of stated criteria.**

A criterion is a stated yardstick — name it, and *better* stops being a feeling and starts being a comparison.

---

## The four criteria

In this fixed order on first mention: **clarity · efficiency · repeatability · error-proneness**.

- **Clarity** — a literal follower can read it once and know what to do.
- **Efficiency** — it reaches the goal without wasted steps.
- **Repeatability** — same input, same result, every time.
- **Error-proneness** — it is hard to do the wrong thing while following it.

Four criteria. Same four names everywhere.

---

## Clarity in two tea recipes

Recipe A — *"Wait 3 minutes, remove the bag."* A number. A literal follower stops at the right time.

Recipe B — *"Wait until the colour looks right, remove the bag."* The follower has to know what *the right colour* looks like. That is one extra unspoken thing.

Both reach the goal. Recipe A is **clearer at step 5** because it names a concrete time.

---

## Efficiency in two backup methods

Method A (USB copy) copies every file one at a time. If the folder has 500 small files, the copy takes longer than the data alone would suggest.

Method B (zip + email) packages the folder into one file first and moves one file once.

Both reach the goal. Method B is **more efficient on a folder with many small files** — fewer wasted steps in the transfer.

---

## Repeatability and error-proneness — quick examples

**Repeatability.** Recipe A always uses 200 ml and three minutes — same drink on Monday and Friday. *"A bit of water, taste it"* gives a different drink every time.

**Error-proneness.** Method A overwrites a previous USB backup if you do not rename the folder; one slip and yesterday's copy is gone. Method B keeps an email-dated copy of every backup — harder to lose by accident.

---

## Trade-offs — when criteria pull in different directions

Recipe A wins on **clarity** (three minutes is precise).

Recipe B may win on **efficiency** (it boils only once and uses the colour as a stopping cue, which a practised tea-drinker reads in a glance).

The "best" choice depends on the follower. **Naming the trade-off is the analysis.** You do not have to crown a winner.

---

## The peer-review protocol

Three steps. In this fixed order.

1. **Criteria** — read the partner's artefact silently. Write one sentence per criterion (clarity, efficiency, repeatability, error-proneness).
2. **Feedback** — pick the **two most useful** sentences. Each names a criterion and a specific step.
3. **Revision** — the author then changes one or two specific things in the original to act on the feedback, and writes a 3–5 sentence change note.

No grades. No numbers. No "best".

---

## Feedback someone can use

Three short rules.

- **Name the criterion.** *"This is unclear"* → *"This is unclear at step 3 because the word *file* does not say which file."*
- **One concrete spot.** *"The whole thing is too long"* → *"Steps 4 and 5 repeat step 2."*
- **Critique the artefact, not the person.**

A suggestion is optional but welcome — *"maybe combine steps 4 and 5"*.
