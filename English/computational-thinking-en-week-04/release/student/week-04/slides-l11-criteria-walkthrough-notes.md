## Slide 1 — Lesson 11 — Criteria walkthrough

Frame the supplement deck. Tell students you are going to apply the four criteria to two specific tea recipes, one criterion at a time. If the L11 whiteboard sketch lands cleanly, skip these slides; if the class wants the slow, side-by-side build, walk through every shape. The point is not tea — it is to feel how each criterion sounds when you actually say it out loud about a real artefact. Ask: "Can two recipes both be right?" That is the whole week in one question. ~2 min.

## Slide 2 — Slide 1 — Worked example: two tea recipes

Set the scene. Two recipes, same goal (a drinkable cup of tea), both valid. We are not going to pick a winner — we are going to look at both through the four criteria. Tell students that this is the exact stance they will take in peer review: name what you see, do not grade it. Concrete IT version: two valid ways to onboard a new user — admin tool first vs. tour first. Same goal, same stance. Ask: "What does it mean for a solution to be valid?" Answer: it actually reaches the goal. ~3 min.

## Slide 3 — Slide 2 — Recipe A (boil-first)

Read all five steps out loud. Notice the order: kettle filled, then boiled, then bag, then pour, then a precise 3-minute wait. The follower never has to make a judgement — every step names a concrete thing or a concrete time. Concrete: this is the recipe you would write on the staffroom wall for new colleagues. Ask: "Which step pins the time down hardest?" Step 5. The "3 minutes" is the clue we will return to under clarity. ~4 min.

## Slide 4 — Slide 3 — Recipe B (steep-first)

Read all five steps. Same number of steps; different order in steps 1–3 (bag goes in first); different stopping cue at step 5 (colour judgement, not clock). Both reach the goal. Tell students this is the recipe a practised tea-drinker would write for themselves — the colour cue is faster than fishing out a timer. Both are real recipes used in real kitchens. Ask: "Which step would change first if a new follower tried Recipe B?" Step 5 — they would not yet know the right colour. ~4 min.

## Slide 5 — Slide 4 — Both reach the goal — but the steps differ

Walk the four bullets. Step order differs. Stopping cue differs. What is left to the follower differs. Number of vessels is the same; number of steps is the same. Land the closing sentence: the differences are real, but they are not errors. This is the line students often fight — they want one of the recipes to be wrong. Concrete: in a code review, two valid implementations of the same function differ in style and order; the reviewer's job is to name the difference, not to call one wrong. Ask: "What makes a difference a real difference and not a mistake?" ~5 min.

## Slide 6 — Slide 5 — The four criteria, side by side

The table is the model. One sentence per cell. No numbers, no grades, no "best". Walk the rows out loud — clarity, efficiency, repeatability, error-proneness. Notice that on efficiency both recipes score the same: one boil, one steep, one cup, no wasted steps. On clarity and repeatability, A has the edge. On error-proneness, it depends on the follower. Tell students this is what the AT2 peer-review table will look like. Ask: "Which cell would you change first?" That is your revision starting point. ~6 min.

## Slide 7 — Slide 6 — The trade-off in plain English

Land the closing message of the deck. A is clearer and more repeatable. B is just as efficient. B may be less error-prone for the practised drinker. The "best" choice depends on the follower — new follower picks A, practised follower picks either. Naming the trade-off is the analysis; no winner is required. Concrete IT version: a new help-desk hire wants the long script; the senior tech wants the short one. Both versions exist in real teams. Ask: "Can you write a one-sentence trade-off about your own Week 2 or Week 3 artefact?" That sentence is gold for AT2. ~5 min.
