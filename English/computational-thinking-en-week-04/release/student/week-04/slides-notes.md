## Slide 1 — Week 4 — Comparing solutions

Open Week 4 by lowering the temperature. Tell students this is a mid-course reflection week — no new computational-thinking primitive, no new vocabulary explosion. They will be looking at their own Week 2 and Week 3 artefacts through new eyes. The new skill is comparing two valid solutions and choosing between them on stated criteria, not on taste. Ask: "Have you ever argued with someone about the right way to do something, where both of you were kind of right?" That is the situation this week trains. ~2 min.

## Slide 2 — The central message

Read the sentence in bold, twice. The two phrases to underline: "more than one valid solution" and "stated criteria". Both halves are the week. Tell students this is the sentence you will quiz them on at the end. The reframe: "I prefer this one" becomes "this one is clearer at step 5". Concrete: a Slack reply that says "I just like option A" tells the team nothing. A reply that says "option A is more repeatable because it uses a fixed timeout" lets the team actually compare. Ask: "What is the difference between an opinion and a criterion?" ~3 min.

## Slide 3 — Where Week 4 sits

Place this week in the eight-week arc. Weeks 1–3 built the primitives. Week 4 is the pause. Weeks 5–8 add code. Tell students this is the calmest week of the course — and the most useful one for AT2, because the peer-review-and-revision habit they train this week is the exact habit AT2 will grade. Concrete: students who skip the peer review here will struggle in Week 6. Ask: "What is one piece of your Week 2 or Week 3 work you would like another pair of eyes on?" That is your peer-review material. ~3 min.

## Slide 4 — A problem is not the same as its solution

The conceptual split. The problem is the goal — a noun phrase, the thing you want at the end. The solution is one way of reaching it — the actual ordered steps. One problem, many possible solutions. This sentence sounds obvious until you watch a student grade themselves on "did I get the right answer" — and discover there is no right answer, just valid and invalid ones. Concrete: "have a recoverable backup" is the problem; the USB copy and the zip-email are two valid solutions. Ask: "What is the problem behind your AT1 task?" Strip it down to a noun phrase. ~4 min.

## Slide 5 — Two ways to make tea

Walk both columns. Both five steps. Both end with a drinkable cup. What differs is the order of the first three steps and the stopping cue at step 5. Notice that "wait 3 minutes" and "wait until the colour looks right" are both valid — one uses a clock, the other uses the eye. We are not crowning a winner yet — we are noticing the differences. Ask: "Which step do you notice differs first?" The answer is usually step 5 — that is the criterion bait. ~5 min.

## Slide 6 — Two ways to back up a project folder

Same shape, vocational version. Two methods, both reach the goal, six steps each. What differs is where the copy ends up living (USB drive vs. email attachment) and what can go wrong (forgotten ejection vs. attachment too big). Tell students both methods are in real use in real IT departments — there is no "professional" answer that beats the other one. Concrete: their school's IT team probably has a preference, and another school's team probably has a different one. Ask: "Which method would you trust more if your laptop was on fire?" That answer surfaces a criterion. ~5 min.

## Slide 7 — What is the same, what is different?

Read the four bullets out loud. Same: both reach the goal, both valid. Different: order, what is left to the follower, what can go wrong. That last list is exactly where the four criteria live. Tell students this slide is the bridge from observation to vocabulary — the next slide names the words for what they just noticed. Ask: "If I said 'option A is just better', what would you ask me to say instead?" Push them toward "better at what?". That question opens criteria. ~4 min.

## Slide 8 — From taste to criterion

The pivot of the week. "I like A better" is taste. "A is clearer at step 5 because three minutes tells the follower exactly when to stop" is a criterion-named comparison. Read the bold sentence one more time. Tell students this is the move they are training: every time they want to say "better", they have to name a criterion. Concrete: in a code review at work, "this is cleaner" gets ignored; "this is clearer because the variable name explains itself" gets acted on. Ask: "Why does naming the criterion change how the other person responds?" ~5 min.

## Slide 9 — The four criteria

Read each one. Memorise the fixed order: clarity, efficiency, repeatability, error-proneness. Tell students this order matters because every peer review they do in this course will use these four names, in this order. Definitions: clarity (read it once, know what to do), efficiency (no wasted steps), repeatability (same input, same result), error-proneness (hard to do wrong). Concrete: a backup script with descriptive filenames scores high on clarity; one that forgets to verify the copy scores high on error-proneness. Ask: "Which of the four is hardest to spot in your own work?" Usually clarity — because you wrote it. ~6 min.

## Slide 10 — Clarity in two tea recipes

Apply criterion one. Recipe A's step 5 names a number; Recipe B's names a colour the follower must already know. Both reach the goal — A is just clearer at step 5. Land the language: not "A is better", but "A is clearer at step 5 because three minutes is a concrete number". Concrete IT version: a help-desk script that says "wait 30 seconds" beats one that says "wait until it looks ready". Ask: "Could B be made clearer without losing its character?" Yes — name the colour. ~5 min.

## Slide 11 — Efficiency in two backup methods

Apply criterion two. Method A copies files one at a time; Method B packages first, then moves one file once. On a folder with many small files, B is more efficient — fewer wasted operations. Notice the language: "more efficient on a folder with many small files". The qualifier matters — it tells the reader when the criterion applies. Concrete: for two big video files, the picture flips — A is fine. Ask: "What does efficiency mean for you in your week?" Time saved. Energy saved. Wrist-pain saved. All valid. ~5 min.

## Slide 12 — Repeatability and error-proneness — quick examples

Apply criteria three and four together. Repeatability: a fixed time (200 ml, three minutes) gives the same drink every day; "a bit of water, taste it" does not. Error-proneness: Method A can overwrite yesterday's backup if you forget to rename; Method B keeps a dated email copy. Concrete IT version: a script with hard-coded values is repeatable but fragile; a script that prompts the user is flexible but error-prone. Ask: "Which of these two criteria feels closer to safety to you?" Most say error-proneness. Both are about reliability. ~5 min.

## Slide 13 — Trade-offs — when criteria pull in different directions

Land the honest message: a solution that wins on one criterion may lose on another. Recipe A wins on clarity; Recipe B can win on efficiency for a practised drinker. Both are valid. Naming the trade-off is the analysis — you do not have to crown a winner. This is the most adult skill of the week. Concrete: at work, "we picked option A because it is clearer for new hires, even though option B saves three seconds per ticket" is a complete answer. Ask: "Why is it OK not to pick a winner?" ~5 min.

## Slide 14 — The peer-review protocol

Three steps, fixed order. Criteria → Feedback → Revision. Silent reading first, then write one sentence per criterion. Pick the two most useful sentences. Then the author revises one or two specific things. No grades, no numbers, no "best". Tell students this is the AT2 protocol in miniature — train it on small artefacts now so it is muscle memory in Week 6. Concrete: code reviews at any tech company follow some version of this exact protocol. Ask: "Why pick only two sentences and not all four?" Answer: focus. ~5 min.

## Slide 15 — Feedback someone can use

The three rules of useful feedback. Name the criterion. Pick one concrete spot. Critique the artefact, not the person. Read each rule and the rewrite that follows. The "this is unclear at step 3 because the word file does not say which file" sentence is the model — students should be able to write one like it by Friday. Concrete: a manager who says "this report is bad" tells the author nothing. A manager who says "the conclusion does not name the recommended action" gives the author exactly what to fix. Ask: "What does the third rule protect?" Answer: the relationship. ~5 min.

## Slide 16 — Tasks this week

One mandatory peer-review task (4.1) on their own Week 2 or Week 3 artefact. Three deepening vocational tasks. Tell students the mandatory task is the one that feeds AT2 directly — do not skip the peer-review-and-revision loop. Concrete: keep the partner's feedback notes and the change note together — AT2 will ask for both. Ask: "Which Week 2 or Week 3 artefact would you bring to peer review?" Pick the one you are least sure about — that is where review pays. ~4 min.

## Slide 17 — What comes next: Week 5 — logic structures

Tease the bridge. Week 5 turns the L7 diamond into formal *if / then / else*, and introduces loops — *do, look, change, do again*. The peer-review habit they trained this week is exactly what AT2 will grade. Concrete: the if/then/else syntax students will meet in Week 5 is just a written form of the diamond from Week 3 — the picture becomes words. Ask: "What is one if/then thing you already do in your day?" If it's raining, take an umbrella. They already have the structure — Week 5 just gives them syntax. ~3 min.

## Slide 18 — Recap — the central message, one more time

Close the loop. Read the bold sentence again. Read the four criteria names in their fixed order. Ask three or four students to give the sentence back in their own words. If they cleanly name "stated criteria" and "valid solutions", the week has landed. Remind them: describe, do not grade. Send them into AT2 prep with the four criteria written on the board. ~3 min.
