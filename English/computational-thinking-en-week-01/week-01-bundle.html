Part 01 · README.md

Course overview

Computational Thinking

A first-year course for vocational ICT students (roughly 15–17 years old, no prior programming experience). The course runs for 8 weeks, with 3 lessons of 90 minutes per week — 24 lessons in total. It begins with everyday instructions a teenager already gives every day, gradually moves through decomposition, abstraction, sequence, condition and repetition, and only in the last two weeks translates that thinking into a tiny browser-based JavaScript program. Programming is treated as the last layer; the thinking is what the course actually trains.

The whole course turns around one central question:

How do I speak to a computer so that it understands what I want it to do?

Part 02 · student/week-01/overview.md

Week 1 — orientation

Week 1 — Speaking to a computer

Week 1 theme

The whole course is built on one question: How do I speak to a computer so that it understands what I want it to do? Across three lessons you will see that a computer follows instructions exactly as written, and that the everyday skill of telling someone how to do something is already a form of computational thinking. Lesson 1 names the problem. Lesson 2 names the everyday tool that solves it — the algorithm. Lesson 3 stress-tests that tool by hunting ambiguity and rewriting.

What you will be able to do by the end of the week

  • State the central question of the course in your own words.
  • Explain that a computer follows instructions literally, and that ambiguity lives in the instruction.
  • Define computational thinking, instruction, algorithm, ambiguity and precision in plain English.
  • Identify the goal, input, steps and output of a familiar activity.
  • Write a short (5–10 step) everyday algorithm in plain English.
  • Find one unclear word in an instruction and rewrite the instruction more precisely.

Core minimum vs. deepening

Core minimum — every student should leave Week 1 able to: - Define the five core terms above in plain English. - Write one short everyday algorithm (for example a recipe for tea, or the route from home to school). - Spot one ambiguous word in a given instruction and propose a clearer version.

Deepening (optional, not required for the assessed task): - Give your instruction to a real follower and rewrite it after watching them get stuck. - Apply the literal-follower lens to a vocational IT scene (a help-desk reply, a README, a chat-bot instruction). - Use an LLM as a sparring partner against your own analysis, with a structured prompt. - Notice and describe one edge case — an unusual situation where a good instruction breaks.

How the three lessons fit together

The three lessons form one arc. Lesson 1 sets the scene: a computer is a literal follower, so the quality of the instruction decides the outcome. Lesson 2 names the artefact good instructions take — the algorithm — and asks you to write your first one. Lesson 3 turns a critical eye on what you wrote: read it as a literal follower would, find the ambiguous words, rewrite. Problem, artefact, stress-test.

Part 03 · student/week-01/self-study.md

Self-study material

Speaking to a computer — what does it actually hear?

Introduction

Imagine your eight-year-old cousin walks into the kitchen and you tell her, "Make me a sandwich." She stares at you. There is bread on the counter, butter in the fridge, cheese somewhere, and a knife in a drawer she is not allowed to open. She has done this with her dad a hundred times, but right now, on her own, she does not know which bread, how thick, with or without crust, and whether the cheese counts. So she takes a slice of bread out of the bag, hands it to you, and asks, "Is this a sandwich?"

She is not confused about what a sandwich is. You just did not give her enough to go on.

This week is about that gap — the small distance between what you mean and what someone else hears. A computer lives in that gap permanently. It does not guess what you meant; it does what you wrote. The whole course you are about to start is built on a single question, and we will come back to it many times:

How do I speak to a computer so that it understands what I want it to do?

The first answer is not "learn a programming language." That comes later in the course. The first answer is: get used to saying exactly what you mean. By the end of this week you will be able to write a clear, step-by-step everyday instruction, hand it to a literal follower, see where it breaks, and fix it. No code, no special tools. Just careful sentences and a willingness to be wrong about how clear you thought you were.

Lesson 1 — Speaking to a computer (literal interpretation and ambiguity)

What is computational thinking?

Computational thinking is the everyday habit of breaking a task down into small, clear, ordered steps that a literal follower can carry out without guessing. It is the thinking behind programming, not programming itself. You are doing it every time you write directions for someone else: "Turn left at the bus stop, walk two blocks, the door is red." Whether the follower is a human or a machine, the same skill applies.

People who study this idea usually name four pillars: decomposition (breaking a big problem into smaller ones), pattern recognition (noticing what repeats), abstraction (focusing on what matters and ignoring the rest), and algorithms (the step-by-step instructions themselves). Each of these gets its own week later in the course. For now, hold on to one short version: computational thinking is the habit of being clear, in steps, for a literal follower.

This is also a good moment to clear up a common confusion. Computational thinking is not the same as programming. Programming is one place you use computational thinking. You can write a perfectly clear algorithm for boiling an egg without touching a keyboard, and the skill you used to do it is exactly the one a programmer uses. The keyboard part comes last.

A computer takes you literally

Let us start away from computers entirely.

Example 1 — the sandwich. You ask your little cousin to make a sandwich. She is willing, she is paying attention, and she trusts you. But she has never made one alone. You said "make me a sandwich" — you did not say which bread, how many slices, what to put inside, or whether to cut it in half. She does the first thing that fits the words you actually said: hands you a slice of bread. She is following you literally. The bread is not a misunderstanding of "sandwich"; it is the part of "sandwich" she was sure about.

Example 2 — the big tree. A friend who has never been to your neighbourhood asks how to get to your house. You say, "Walk down my street, turn at the big tree, and it's the third door on the left." Three weeks later she tells you she got lost. Why? Because to you "the big tree" is obvious — it is the one on the corner with the white trunk — but to her every tree on that street looked roughly the same size. "Big" was not a measurement; it was a memory you had and she didn't.

Now the IT side.

Example 3 — the typo search. You want a Python tutorial and you type pyhton tutorial into a search engine. The search engine does not say "I think you mean Python." (Modern ones do, because they have been trained to guess, but the raw matching layer underneath does not.) At the literal level, it is looking for pages that contain pyhton, a word that does not exist, and gives you whatever comes closest. It is doing exactly what you typed.

Example 4 — the missing slash. A program tries to open a file at Users/maria/documentsreport.txt instead of Users/maria/documents/report.txt. To you the difference is one slash; to the computer it is the difference between a folder it can find and a filename it has never heard of. It does not try harder; it reports an error.

In all four examples there is no machine that is broken and no follower at fault. There is an instruction, and the instruction was less clear than the speaker thought.

Ambiguity is in the instruction, not the computer

This is the conceptual pivot of the whole week, so it is worth saying slowly: ambiguity is a property of the instruction, not of the follower. When your cousin handed you a slice of bread, the ambiguity was in "make me a sandwich." When your friend got lost, the ambiguity was in "the big tree." When the search engine returned junk for pyhton, the ambiguity was in the typed word.

This matters because the cultural reflex is the opposite. When an instruction fails, people often blame the machine and say it does not understand them. That framing is wrong, and once you let go of it, the whole course gets easier. The computer is literal. It does exactly what is written, and that is actually its strength — if you can write a clear instruction, you can rely on the computer to follow it the same way every time.

So when something goes wrong, the question you learn to ask is not "why is this machine not getting it?" but "where in my instruction was I unclear?" That second question has answers. The first one does not.

Why this matters for an IT professional

You might be wondering why a first-year vocational ICT student needs to think about sandwiches and trees. Here is the short version: an IT professional spends most of their day writing instructions. Instructions for machines (when you eventually write code). Instructions for colleagues (a ticket, a handover note, a step-by-step fix). Instructions for end users (a how-to guide, a chat-bot response, a help-desk reply, a README). All of these have the same shape, and all of them break in the same way: a word that seems obvious to you turns out to be unclear to the person on the other side.

When a customer writes "it doesn't work" in a support ticket, they are giving you an unclear instruction. When you write back "have you tried restarting?" without saying what to restart, you are giving them one. The whole field runs on small clear sentences, and the people who write them well stand out fast.

Pause for a moment: Think of one time this week when you gave a clear instruction to a friend, a sibling, or a parent — and one time when you gave an unclear one. What was the difference? Was it a missing word, an assumption you made about what they already knew, or a number you did not specify?

Lesson 2 — Algorithms in everyday life

What is an algorithm?

An algorithm is a clear sequence of literal steps that takes you from where you are to a goal. That is the whole definition. It does not have to involve a computer. It does not have to be long. It does not have to use any special words. A grandmother's recipe written on a card is an algorithm. The mental checklist you go through when you leave the house is an algorithm. The route your bus takes is an algorithm. If a literal follower can carry out the steps and reach the goal, you have an algorithm.

The word sounds technical because we usually hear it next to "algorithm change at YouTube" or "TikTok's algorithm decided." Those algorithms are made of code and are very large, but they are still, at root, sequences of clear steps. The algorithm you are about to write yourself is the same thing, just shorter.

Algorithms you already use

Here are three algorithms you almost certainly run every day without thinking of them that way.

A recipe — tea with milk. Goal: a hot mug of tea with milk. Input: a tea bag, water, milk, sugar (optional), a mug, a kettle. Steps: fill the kettle with cold water, switch the kettle on, put a tea bag in the mug, wait for the kettle to boil, pour the water into the mug over the tea bag, wait three minutes, take the tea bag out, add a splash of milk, add sugar if you want, stir. Output: a hot mug of tea with milk.

Notice four things in that recipe: a goal (what you are trying to end up with), an input (what you start with), a sequence of steps, and an output (what you actually get when the algorithm finishes). Almost every algorithm you meet — everyday or in IT — has these four parts.

A route — home to school. Goal: arrive at school on time. Input: you, your bag, your bus card, the time on the kitchen clock, your front door. Steps: leave the house by 7:50, walk to the bus stop on Park Street, take bus 12 in the direction of the city centre, get off at the stop called Market, walk two blocks east, enter through the main gate. Output: you, inside school, on time.

A morning routine. Goal: leave the house ready for the day. Input: you, asleep, in bed; an alarm; clothes; breakfast in the kitchen. Steps: alarm goes off, switch it off, get out of bed, shower, get dressed, eat breakfast, brush teeth, pack bag, put on shoes, leave. Output: you, dressed, fed, on the way out.

None of these are written in code. They do not need to be. They are still algorithms because each of them describes a clear sequence of literal steps from a known input to a known output.

The same shape appears in an IT context too. Picture the first-line help-desk script for "the user cannot log in." Goal: get the user logged in. Input: the username, the error message they saw, the computer they are on. Steps: ask the user to type their username slowly while you watch, check whether caps lock is on, ask them to try once more, if it still fails reset their password, send the new one to their personal email, ask them to log in with the new password, ask them to change it on first login. Output: a logged-in user and a closed ticket. Same shape: goal, input, steps, output.

Pause for a moment: Look at the morning-routine algorithm above. If you had to give it to a literal follower — a robot vacuum cleaner with arms, say — which step would break first? Is it "get out of bed"? "Shower"? "Pack bag"? Pick one and write down, in one sentence, why a literal follower would get stuck there.

Two algorithms for the same goal

A common mistake when people first meet the word "algorithm" is to think there is one correct algorithm per goal, and your job is to find it. Not true. For most everyday goals there are several valid algorithms, and all of them work.

Take the tea recipe again. Some people put the milk in first and pour the hot water on top. Some people put the milk in last, after the bag has steeped. Both end with a hot mug of tea with milk in it. Both are correct. They are different algorithms for the same goal.

Or take the route from home to school. You can walk to the bus stop on Park Street and take bus 12. Or you can walk fifteen extra minutes and take the tram instead. Or, on a sunny day, you can cycle. Three different algorithms, same goal: you, inside school, on time.

This matters because later in the course we will start asking, "is one of these algorithms better than another?" — and to ask that question you first have to accept that more than one of them can be valid. For now: notice when you and a friend solve the same everyday problem differently. You both have working algorithms. Neither of you is wrong.

Writing your first algorithm in plain English

Before you read on, try this. On a piece of paper or in a notes app, write out the algorithm you follow when you make a cup of tea, or get yourself ready in the morning, or send a Discord message to a friend. Use plain English. Number the steps. Try to write between five and ten steps. Before you start, write the goal at the top. Before the steps, write what you start with (the input). After the steps, write what you end up with (the output).

If you find yourself writing a step like "do the usual thing with the kettle," stop and break it into smaller, more literal steps. A literal follower does not know what "the usual thing" is.

This is the same activity you will turn into Task 1.1 of the week's tasks. Do not worry about being elegant. Worry about being clear.

Pause for a moment: When you wrote your steps, were there any places where you almost wrote a word like "usually," "normally," "the right one," or "the one you always use"? Those words are warning lights. A literal follower has no "usually." Circle them.

Lesson 3 — Precise and imprecise instructions

What does "imprecise" mean here?

An imprecise (or ambiguous) instruction is one that more than one literal follower could carry out in more than one way and still believe they followed it. Notice that the definition does not say anything about length. A very short instruction can be perfectly clear ("close the door"). A very long one can be hopelessly unclear if it keeps using vague words.

Here is a short instruction. Read it once and ask yourself how many different things a literal follower might do with it: "Save the file in the usual place."

What is "the file"? The one you have open? The one you were just looking at five minutes ago? The one your colleague mentioned in the chat? What is "the usual place"? Your desktop? The shared drive? The folder called inbox that you keep meaning to clean out? Six words and several possible meanings.

A clearer version might be: "Save the file report-may.docx (currently open in Word) into the shared drive folder Team/Reports/2026/May." The new version is longer, but it leaves only one thing for the follower to do.

Hunting ambiguity

Ambiguity in everyday English tends to come in a few recognisable shapes. Once you know what to look for, you start spotting it everywhere.

Vague nouns. "Pass me the thing." "Open the file." "Reply to the email." In each of these, the noun (thing, file, email) is not specific enough to point at one object. Fix: name the object directly, or describe it in a way only one object fits.

Deictic words (words that depend on where the speaker is). "Put it here." "Take that over there." "Send it to him." Here, there, it, him all depend on context that the follower may not share. Fix: replace the deictic word with the actual location, object or person.

Relative time. "Run the script after lunch." "I'll do it later." "Send it soon." After lunch, later, soon mean different things to different people and on different days. Fix: name a specific time, or a specific event with no ambiguity ("after the 14:00 stand-up").

Vague quantities. "Add a bit of milk." "Make the button a bit bigger." "Reply to a few of them." A bit, some, a few are ranges, not numbers. Fix: give a number, a measurement, or a clear cap ("add 30 ml of milk," "make the button 24 pixels tall," "reply to the first three").

The same four patterns show up in IT contexts almost word for word. Vague nouns: "save it in the folder" (which folder?). Deictic: "log in here" (here on which page?). Relative time: "the deployment will go out later today" (before or after the 16:00 demo?). Vague quantities: "the upload is taking a while" (a minute? an hour?).

When you are reading an instruction someone else wrote — or one you wrote yourself five minutes ago — going through these four patterns in your head is one of the fastest ambiguity hunts you can do.

Testing instructions by acting as the literal computer

There is one technique that beats every other technique for finding ambiguity in your own instruction: test it by acting as the literal follower.

Reading your own instruction is not enough. When you wrote it, you knew what you meant. So when you read it back, you read what you meant, not what you wrote. Your brain fills in the missing words automatically. The only way to break that habit is to follow the instruction one step at a time, with no memory of what you meant, the way a robot would. At each step, you ask: "Could I do this in more than one way? Did the previous step give me what I need to do this one?"

This is what Task 1.1 of the week's tasks is about. In Task 1.1 you write an instruction, hand it to a real follower, and watch them get stuck. In the in-class "literal robot" exercise (Lesson 3, second half) you do this live with a classmate playing the literal computer.

When you do this for the first time, you will be surprised. Instructions you were sure were clear turn out to have three or four spots where the follower has to guess. That is normal. Every IT professional discovers it the first time they write a how-to guide for a real user. The fix is never to write a longer instruction; it is to write a more literal one.

Edge cases (a gentle introduction)

An instruction can be perfectly clear for the common case and still break in an unusual one. The unusual situation is called an edge case.

Take a tiny instruction: "Open the fridge and take out the milk." If there is milk in the fridge, the instruction works. But what if there is no milk? What if there are two cartons and one is past its date? What if the carton is in the door instead of on the shelf? These are edge cases. They do not mean the instruction is bad. They mean the instruction does not say what to do when the world is not in the shape the writer expected.

A more robust version might say: "Open the fridge. If there is a carton of milk on the shelf, take it out. If there is more than one, take the one with the earliest date. If there is none, tell me."

You do not need to anticipate every edge case in Week 1. You just need to start noticing that they exist. Later in the course (especially in Week 5, when you meet "if/then/else" thinking), you will learn to handle them on purpose. For now, when you write an algorithm, ask yourself once: "What if the world is not in the shape I expected? What would my follower do then?"

Pause for a moment: Pick the morning-routine algorithm you wrote (or were going to write) in Lesson 2. Find one edge case — a situation where one of the steps would not work as written. (No clothes you like? No hot water? Alarm did not go off?) Write down, in one sentence, what your follower should do in that situation.

Summary and what comes next

You started this week with a question: How do I speak to a computer so that it understands what I want it to do? You now have a first answer in three parts.

First, a computer follows instructions literally. The computer is exact, not creative. When something goes wrong, the question is not what is wrong with the machine, but where you were unclear.

Second, an algorithm is the everyday name for a clear sequence of literal steps. You use algorithms every day without thinking. The skill you will spend this course developing is the skill of writing algorithms that a literal follower can actually carry out.

Third, instructions are not finished when they sound right. They are finished when a literal follower can execute them without asking questions. Testing your instruction — by handing it to a real follower, or by acting as the literal follower yourself — is the only reliable way to find the unclear parts.

In Week 2 we will keep the literal-follower mindset but tackle a new problem: what do you do when the task is too big to fit in a single algorithm? The answer is decomposition — breaking a problem into smaller problems you can solve one at a time. The instructions you have written this week have been small enough that decomposition has not been needed. Next week they get bigger.

Keep your notes from this week's tasks. You will reuse them, especially in the first assessed task at the end of Week 2.

Part 04 · student/week-01/student-tasks.md

Student tasks

Week 1 — Student tasks

Overview of the week's tasks

This week has four tasks. The task with the green dot (🟢 MANDATORY) is the must-do minimum — finish it to keep up with the class. The tasks with the purple dot (🟣 DEEPENING) are optional extensions: do one or more of them if you want to go further or if a teacher asks you to.

Keep every note, draft and rewrite in a single document or notebook for the week. The first assessed task (AT1) lands at the end of Week 2, and you will reuse Week 1 material — your own instructions, your ambiguous words, your rewrites — as raw material for AT1. Throw nothing away.

A reminder before you start: in this course the computer is literal, not stupid. Every task below is a chance to feel that difference in your own hands.

Task 1.1 — The literal robot gameMANDATORY

Goal: Feel in your own hands what "a computer follows instructions literally" actually means. You write a short instruction for a task you really do, hand it to a real follower, watch what breaks, and rewrite it.

This is the most important task of the week. Everything else builds on it.

Steps

  1. Pick a task you personally do almost every day. Good choices: making a cup of tea or coffee, logging in to the school portal on your phone, sending a message on Discord or WhatsApp, opening a specific app on your laptop, charging your phone. Pick one. Do not pick something you have only read about — pick something your hands already know how to do.
  2. Write a 6–10 step instruction in plain English. Number every step. Each step should be one short sentence. Do not skip "obvious" things; if your hands do it, write it. Do not use code, arrows, or symbols — only English.
  3. Find a real follower. A classmate, a sibling, a parent, a friend. If you truly cannot find anyone, record yourself on video doing exactly what your instructions say, no more and no less — this counts as a follower too.
  4. Brief the follower with one sentence only: "Do exactly what the instructions say. Do nothing they do not say. If a step is unclear, stop and tell me which word is unclear." Then hand them the instruction and stay quiet. Do not coach. Do not gesture. Watch.
  5. Write down at least 3 places the follower got stuck. For each, note (a) the step number, (b) the exact word or phrase that was ambiguous, and (c) what the follower did instead of what you intended.
  6. Rewrite the instruction. Fix the three ambiguities. Add steps if you have to. The new instruction can be longer; that is fine.
  7. Re-test with the same follower. Did the new version work? Did new problems appear that were hidden by the old ones?

If you are doing this alone

If you cannot find a follower, here is the alternative: write your instruction, then leave it for at least 24 hours. Come back the next day and do exactly what the words say, with no memory of what you meant — pretend a colleague handed it to you. The 24-hour gap forces you to read what is actually on the page, not what is in your head. Note the same three breakdowns and rewrite as in steps 5–7. Optionally, you can also film yourself doing the instruction once, then watch the recording the next day with fresh eyes.

Reflection (3–5 sentences)

Answer in your notes:

  • Which one word or step was the most ambiguous, and why?
  • Were you surprised by anything the follower did (or by your future self)? Why was it surprising?
  • If a computer (not a human) had to follow your final version, would it still get stuck somewhere? Where?

Tip: The follower is doing you a favour by being literal. If they "helpfully" guessed what you meant, the test does not work. Ask them again — kindly — to take every word at face value.

Keep your notes

Keep your final instruction, the three ambiguities, and your reflection. AT1 at the end of Week 2 will ask you to apply the same literal-follower lens to a slightly bigger problem. You will reuse this work.

Task 1.2 — Spot the ambiguityDEEPENING

Goal: Train your eye to see ambiguity in short, realistic instructions — the kind you read every day in messages, tickets and notes.

Collect your own five

Over the next two to three days, collect five ambiguous instructions you have actually received or given this week — in a chat, an email, a classroom announcement, a help-desk reply, a sticky note on the fridge, a WhatsApp from a parent. Do not invent them and do not borrow them from a textbook. They must be real instructions from your own life this week.

For each of the five, write in your notes:

  • The instruction itself, copied word for word.
  • One sentence of context: where it was, who said it to whom, and the situation.
  • (a) which word or phrase is ambiguous,
  • (b) at least two different things a literal follower might do, and
  • (c) a rewritten version that removes the ambiguity.

The primary artefact for this task is your own collection of five real, contextualised instructions — not the annotation alone.

Optional sparring-partner step

Once your five instructions and your own analysis are written down, you may push your thinking further by pasting your annotations into an LLM (such as ChatGPT or Claude) using the prompt below. Do not accept the LLM's answer as the truth. Treat it as a sparring partner that pokes at your thinking.

You are my sparring partner, not my teacher. I am a vocational ICT
student learning to spot ambiguity in everyday instructions.

Below are five short instructions and my own annotation for each:
what is ambiguous, what a literal follower might do, and my rewrite.

[paste your annotations here]

Please challenge me. For each instruction:
- Point out at least one ambiguity I missed, if any.
- Question my rewrite: is the new version still ambiguous?
- Do not give me the "right" answer outright. Ask me a question
  that helps me see the gap myself.

After the LLM responds, decide for each suggestion whether to accept, modify or reject it. Write one short sentence next to each suggestion explaining your decision. The point of the task is your decisions, not the LLM's text.

Tip: A rewrite that "sounds clearer" can still be ambiguous. Read your rewrite aloud and ask: could a literal follower still pick the wrong meaning?

Keep your notes

Keep your five real instructions (with their context), your annotations and your rewrites. AT1 lands at the end of Week 2, and these five contextualised examples are exactly the kind of raw material AT1 will ask you to draw on. Throw none of them away.

Task 1.3 — Two algorithms, one goalDEEPENING

Goal: Notice that more than one valid algorithm can reach the same goal, and that "better" depends on what you care about.

Steps

  1. Pick one goal you can reach in more than one way. Examples: getting from home to the school's main entrance, making a cup of tea, charging your phone from a flat battery, looking up the weather for tomorrow. Pick a goal you have at least two real ways of reaching.
  2. Write Algorithm A. Number the steps (5–10 steps). At the top of the algorithm, write four short lines: - Goal: what the algorithm accomplishes - Input: what you need before you start - Steps: (the numbered list) - Output: what you have at the end
  3. Write Algorithm B for the same goal. Same structure. Different steps. Not "the same algorithm with different words" — a genuinely different way to reach the same goal.
  4. Compare them in 3–5 sentences. Which one is shorter? Which one is easier to follow? Which one is easier to mess up? Which one would you give to someone who has never done this task before, and why?

There is no single right answer here. The point is that you can say, in plain English, why one is more suitable for a given situation.

Tip: If Algorithm B feels like Algorithm A in disguise, force a difference: change the starting input (e.g. start from a cold kettle versus a hot one) or swap the order of two early steps.

Keep your notes

Keep both algorithms and the comparison. AT1 lands at the end of Week 2 (start of Week 3) and will reuse this "more than one valid algorithm" thinking as raw material — your notes here will save you time. Week 4 will then return to "more than one correct answer" in a more formal way.

Task 1.4 — Vocational angle: write a help-desk instructionDEEPENING

Goal: Apply the literal-follower lens to a realistic IT task. Write an instruction that a real first-line help-desk agent could follow to walk a user through one specific thing in your school's IT context.

Steps

  1. Pick one concrete task from your school's IT environment. Examples: "change your school password from a school laptop", "connect your phone to the school Wi-Fi", "find your timetable in the school portal", "join a Teams meeting from your phone for the first time". It must be a task that actually exists in your school — you should be able to walk over to a computer and do it.
  2. Write the instruction. 8–15 numbered steps, plain English, one short sentence per step. Include the exact name of buttons, menu items and pages as they appear in your school's software. If a step depends on something (for example, you must be on the school Wi-Fi first), say so.
  3. Find a real tester. A classmate who has not done this specific task before is ideal. Ask them to follow your instruction literally and to stop and tell you any word that is unclear. Watch quietly.
  4. List 3 places it broke down. What was the ambiguity? Was it a vague word, a missing step, or an assumption that the tester already knew something?
  5. Rewrite and re-test once. Note what improved.

If you are doing this alone

If you cannot find a tester, here is the alternative: post the instruction in your class chat or course platform with one line: "Trying this for a class task — would anyone try it on their own device and tell me which step they got stuck on?" If that is not an option either, use the 24-hour gap technique: write the instruction today, come back tomorrow with no notes, and execute it as if a colleague had handed it to you. You can also walk through the instruction on a different device than the one you wrote it on (your phone instead of your laptop, or a classroom PC instead of your home setup) — the change of context will surface assumptions you did not notice. Document the same three breakdowns either way.

Reflection (3–5 sentences)

  • What kind of ambiguity showed up most: vague names ("the menu"), missing context ("if you are on the school Wi-Fi"), or assumed prior knowledge?
  • How is writing for an end user different from writing for yourself?
  • Which one of your steps would you keep exactly as it is, even if a colleague asked you to shorten it? Why?

Tip: A great help-desk instruction names what the user sees on the screen at every step. If the tester ever has to ask "where is that?", you have skipped naming something the screen actually shows.

Keep your notes

Keep your final help-desk instruction. AT1 (end of Week 2) will ask you to decompose a slightly larger IT task; your help-desk instruction is exactly the right size of raw material to build on.

Vocabulary

Vocabulary

Week 1 vocabulary

The terms below cover Lessons 1, 2 and 3. Definitions are short on purpose — read the self-study for examples in context. One term at the end (decomposition) is a preview of Week 2, flagged as such.

Computational thinking

The everyday habit of breaking a task into small, clear, ordered steps that a literal follower can carry out without guessing. It is the thinking behind programming, not programming itself. Writing directions to a friend's house is already a form of computational thinking.

Instruction

A sentence or step that tells a follower what to do. An instruction can be addressed to a person, to a machine, or to yourself. In this course, "instruction" always means something written or spoken that a follower is expected to act on.

Computer (as instruction-follower)

For the purposes of this course, a computer is best understood as a follower that carries out instructions exactly as they are written, without guessing what the writer probably meant. The computer is literal, not creative. Its strength is that it behaves the same way every time, as long as the instruction is the same.

Literal interpretation

Following an instruction word by word, taking nothing for granted and adding nothing the instruction did not say. A computer always interprets literally. A human follower can also interpret literally on purpose — for example, during the "literal robot" exercise.

Ambiguity

A property of an instruction (not of the follower) where more than one literal follower could reasonably carry it out in more than one way. "Save the file in the usual place" is ambiguous because both "the file" and "the usual place" can mean several different things.

Algorithm

A clear sequence of literal steps that takes a known input and produces a known output, reaching a stated goal. A recipe, a morning routine, and the route from home to school are all algorithms. Algorithms do not need code; they just need to be clear enough that a literal follower can execute them.

Goal

What an algorithm is trying to achieve — the end state. The goal of a tea recipe is "a hot mug of tea." Naming the goal first helps you check, at the end, whether your algorithm actually reaches it.

Input

What an algorithm starts with — the things, information or conditions the follower needs before they can begin. For a tea recipe the input is the kettle, the water, the tea bag, the mug, and (optionally) milk and sugar.

Step

One single action in an algorithm. A good step is small enough that a literal follower can carry it out without asking a clarifying question. "Switch the kettle on" is a step. "Make tea" is not — it is a whole algorithm.

Output

What the follower ends up with when the algorithm finishes. The output is checked against the goal: if they match, the algorithm worked.

Precision

The quality of an instruction (or a step) that leaves a literal follower with exactly one reasonable way to carry it out. "Add 30 ml of milk" is precise. "Add a bit of milk" is not. Precision is the main thing this week trains you to look for.

Imprecise (instruction)

An instruction that is not precise — one that uses vague nouns, relative time, vague quantities, or words like here, there, it that depend on context the follower may not share. Imprecise instructions are not always wrong; they just leave room for the follower to guess.

Test (an instruction)

Trying out an instruction with a literal follower (a classmate, a family member, yourself acting like a robot) to see where it breaks. Testing is the fastest way to find ambiguity, because the follower's hesitation points straight at the unclear words. Re-reading your own instruction does not work as well — you read what you meant, not what you wrote.

Edge case

An unusual situation where an otherwise good instruction does not say what to do. "Open the fridge and take out the milk" has an edge case: what if there is no milk? Noticing edge cases is a Week 1 starting point; handling them on purpose comes later in the course.

Literal-robot game

The classroom and at-home technique in which one person reads an instruction and another follows it literally, taking nothing for granted, in order to find where the instruction was unclear. The technique gives its name to Task 1.1 and to the in-class exercise in Lesson 3.

Decomposition (looking ahead — central topic of Week 2)

Breaking a problem that is too big for one algorithm into smaller problems that each have their own small algorithm. You have not needed decomposition this week because the instructions have been short; in Week 2 they get longer, and decomposition becomes the next core skill.

Teacher materials

Week 1 — Teacher materials

This document covers all three lessons of Week 1 (L1, L2, L3) in one place. It is built around the same central question the students see: "How do I speak to a computer so that it understands what I want it to do?" Week 1 establishes the conceptual foundation for the whole 8-week course. No code, no flowcharts, no pseudocode is introduced this week; the three lessons sit together as a single arc — Lesson 1 names the problem (literal interpretation, ambiguity), Lesson 2 names the artefact that solves it (the algorithm), and Lesson 3 stress-tests the artefact by hunting and removing ambiguity.

Cross-references: the in-class activities used in Lesson 1 and Lesson 3 are described in detail in in-class-tasks.md in the same folder. Cross-references appear in the per-lesson plans below.

Week 1 learning objectives (mapped to Bloom)

Week 1 stays inside Remembering and Understanding, with light Applying allowed in Lessons 2 and 3 (writing a short everyday algorithm; rewriting an imprecise instruction). The forbidden verbs for Week 1 objectives are create, build, design, develop, evaluate, judge, analyze, decompose, abstract, debug. These return in later weeks.

Lesson 1 — Humans, computers, and interpreting instructions

  • Remembering: the student can define computational thinking in their own words.
  • Understanding: the student can explain that a computer follows instructions literally.
  • Understanding: the student can give one everyday example and one IT example of "speaking to a computer".
  • Understanding: the student can recognize that ambiguity is a property of the instruction, not of the computer.

Lesson 2 — Algorithms in everyday life

  • Remembering: the student can define algorithm in their own words.
  • Understanding: the student can identify algorithms in everyday situations (recipe, route, morning routine).
  • Understanding: the student can describe the four parts of an algorithm — goal, input, steps, output.
  • Understanding: the student can compare two algorithms for the same goal at a surface level (which is shorter, which is easier to follow).
  • Light Applying: the student can write a 5–10 step everyday algorithm in plain English.

Lesson 3 — Precise and imprecise instructions

  • Understanding: the student can identify ambiguous words in a short instruction.
  • Understanding: the student can explain why testing reveals defects faster than re-reading.
  • Light Applying: the student can rewrite an imprecise instruction so a literal follower can execute it.
  • Light Applying: the student can test an instruction by acting as the "literal computer".

The course's higher Bloom levels — Analyzing (Week 4), Creating (final assessed task, Week 8) — are deliberately not in scope this week.

Common misconceptions for Week 1

At least two misconceptions per lesson appear here. Pre-empt the first one on the list (the computer is stupid) at the very start of Lesson 1 — it is the most common and most damaging if left.

Lesson 1 misconceptions

Misconception: "The computer is stupid." Correction: The computer is literal, not stupid. A literal follower has no opinion about whether an instruction makes sense; it does exactly what the words allow. Insist on the word literal from minute one and gently correct stupid/dumb whenever it appears in student speech. The whole course rests on this reframing — students who keep "stupid" in their vocabulary will keep assuming the machine should have known what they meant.

Misconception: "Computational thinking is just programming." Correction: Computational thinking is the thinking behind programming — taking a problem, naming what is fixed and what varies, and expressing the solution as clear steps. Programming is one place where computational thinking is used, not the only one. A recipe, a help-desk script and a fire-drill procedure are all forms of computational thinking. Programming arrives properly in Week 7.

Lesson 2 misconceptions

Misconception: "Algorithms are only for computer scientists." Correction: Every recipe is an algorithm. Every set of bus directions is an algorithm. Every morning routine is an algorithm. The word algorithm names something students already do — the lesson is giving them a name for the skill they already have, not teaching a brand-new skill.

Misconception: "An algorithm must be long to count." Correction: Three clear steps can be a valid algorithm if they reach the goal without ambiguity. Length is not the criterion; clarity and reaching the goal are. Conversely, a 30-step instruction full of vague words is a poor algorithm.

Lesson 3 misconceptions

Misconception: "Imprecise just means short." Correction: A long instruction can still be imprecise. A short instruction can be perfectly precise. Imprecision is about whether a literal follower has more than one defensible thing to do, not about how many words there are.

Misconception: "If the instruction sounds right when I read it, it is right." Correction: Reading your own instruction back to yourself rarely surfaces ambiguity, because you already know what you meant. Only testing with a literal follower (a classmate, a sibling, or yourself reading it 24 hours later) reveals the gaps. This is why Lesson 3's central activity is to act as the literal computer.

Misconception (bonus, often appears in Lesson 1 or 3): "If I add 'please' or 'kindly', the computer will be nicer." Correction: A literal follower has no manners channel. Please and kindly are no more or less informative than the verbs around them; they neither add nor subtract literal meaning. This is also a useful moment to point out that human politeness conventions do not transfer to literal instruction-following.

Facilitation tips

Before any of the three classes

  • Test the central question on yourself first. Read it aloud once, slowly. If you cannot say it from memory by the time you walk into class, students will not absorb it either. The verbatim form — "How do I speak to a computer so that it understands what I want it to do?" — appears on the slide deck twice; mirror that in your speech.
  • Pre-write the board layout. For Lesson 1, sketch the "Everyday vs. IT" two-column grid in your notes. For Lesson 2, sketch the four parts of an algorithm. For Lesson 3, sketch the four ambiguity patterns. Drawing this on the board live is fine; thinking about it live is risky.
  • Read in-class-tasks.md end-to-end before Lesson 1. Activity A appears twice during the week and needs slips of paper prepared. If you are co-teaching, hand off Activity A explicitly — it works better when one person owns it.

During class

  • Lead with the everyday example. Always. The brief is firm: the everyday example before the IT example. If a student offers an IT example first, accept it but pair it with an everyday one before moving on.
  • Insist on the word literal. Every time a student says the computer is stupid, gently swap the word: "Literal — the computer is literal. Stupid is when you should have known better. The computer never had a chance to know." Two or three corrections in Lesson 1 are usually enough for the rest of the week.
  • Hold silence after handing the slip to the volunteer. New teachers tend to narrate the volunteer's actions. Do not. The silence is the lesson.
  • Use the board for rewrites, not the slides. Slides set up the question; rewrites must happen on the board so the class sees the live transition from ambiguous to precise.
  • Watch the clock for the 30–50% rule. Independent work is the part teachers under-pressure cut first. Protect it. The schedules above leave 30–35 minutes per lesson; do not trim below 27.

Answers to common student questions

  • "Is Google an algorithm?" — Yes, Google search is implemented by algorithms (many of them, including modern machine-learning ones). For Week 1, the answer the student needs is: yes, an algorithm in the everyday sense — a sequence of steps that takes an input (your query) and produces an output (a list of results). Deeper detail belongs in later weeks.
  • "Why doesn't autocorrect understand me?" — Because autocorrect is also literal in its own way: it follows rules about which letter sequences look like which words, and it has no idea what you meant. Use this as a bridge to "ambiguity is in the instruction, not the computer".
  • "Is this on the assessed task?" — Week 1 has no assessed task. AT1 lands at the end of Week 2. The notes students make this week are the raw material for AT1; that is the honest answer.
  • "Do I need a computer to do this homework?" — No. Tasks 1.1 and 1.4 can be done with paper, a pen and a willing follower. Tasks 1.2 and 1.3 only need paper. The only "computer" Week 1 needs is the literal one inside the classroom.
  • "Can I use ChatGPT?" — Yes, in the way Task 1.2 describes — as a sparring partner that pokes at your thinking. You may not paste its output and submit it. Task 1.1 in particular cannot be faked by an LLM, because it requires a real follower and an observable artefact.

Formative assessment tips

Week 1 has no summative assessment. The role of formative checks is to make student understanding visible early, so misconceptions can be corrected before Week 2's decomposition layer is added on top. Use one or two of the following per lesson; do not try to use all of them.

Per-lesson informal checks

  • Lesson 1 — exit ticket (3 min, end of class). Each student writes one sentence answering: "In your own words, what does it mean that a computer is literal?" Collect the tickets, skim them on the way to Lesson 2, and start Lesson 2 by reading two anonymous strong answers aloud. Wrong answers are not corrected publicly; instead, the misconception list above tells you what to revisit in Lesson 2's warm-up.
  • Lesson 1 — board vote. Show three statements: (a) "The computer is stupid." (b) "The computer is literal." (c) "It depends." Have each student raise a hand for one. The pattern of hands tells you whether the central reframing landed; a strong (a) or (c) vote means revisit before moving on.
  • Lesson 2 — pair report-back (2 min, after the pair activity). Ask two pairs to name one part of their partner's algorithm that was clear and one that was vague. Listen for whether students are using the words goal, input, steps, output — that is the success signal.
  • Lesson 2 — one-minute paper. End of class: "Write down one algorithm you used today that you didn't think of as an algorithm before this lesson." Read three at the start of Lesson 3 as a warm-up.
  • Lesson 3 — sticky-note collection. Activity A already ends with sticky notes; keep them. The pattern of ambiguous instructions students write is itself an assessment of how well they recognise ambiguity in their own language.
  • Lesson 3 — exit ticket. "Name the ambiguity pattern you find easiest to spot, and the one you find hardest." Use answers to choose Week 2's warm-up examples.

What you are listening for across the week

The two phrases that mark genuine understanding by the end of Week 1 are:

  • "The instruction is ambiguous" (rather than the computer is wrong).
  • "Let me test it with a literal follower" (rather than let me re-read it).

If those two phrases appear in student speech by Lesson 3, Week 1 has done its job and Week 2 (decomposition) can build on it without remediation.

This file contains the in-class, teacher-led activities for Week 1. They run during class, not as homework.

  • Activity A — "Literal robot live" is MANDATORY. It belongs in the 55–80 min slot of Lesson 3 and is previewed as a 5-min teacher demo at the start of Lesson 1.
  • Activity B — "Algorithm relay (pair work)" is OPTIONAL. It fits the 60–80 min slot of Lesson 2, or serves as a Lesson 3 warm-up if Activity A has to be shortened.

Both require live classroom interaction — a volunteer playing the computer, classmates noticing ambiguity in real time, the teacher writing rewrites on the board. They cannot be replaced by homework or by an LLM.

Activity A — "Literal robot live"MANDATORY

Goal. Students see, in their own classroom, what literal interpretation looks like — a person taking ambiguous instructions at face value and producing a result no one expected. Makes the central message of Week 1 physically visible.

Group size. Whole class (one volunteer at a time, the rest observes and contributes).

Time required. 25 min (Lesson 3, 55–80 min slot). A 5-min teacher demo at the start of Lesson 1 uses the same setup with a single instruction.

Teacher preparation (before class).

  1. Prepare three short ambiguous instructions on slips of paper. Suggested set: "Get me a chair." / "Open the window a bit." / "Write your name on the board."
  2. Have a board marker, the board, and at least one spare chair in the room.
  3. Decide where the "stage area" is — usually between the board and the first row.
  4. A pad of sticky notes per row for the closing minute.

Step-by-step facilitation.

  1. Set the rules (2 min). "In a moment one of you volunteers as 'the computer'. The computer does only what the words say — no guessing, no kind interpretations. If two readings are possible, the computer may pick the one you find funny. The audience's job is to spot the ambiguity." Make clear the role is the joke, not the volunteer.
  2. Hand slip #1 to the first volunteer (1 min). Step back. Stay quiet.
  3. Watch (1–2 min). They may bring a chair, slide their own, take one from a classmate, stand on one. Let the moment land. Do not narrate.
  4. Debrief (3 min). Ask: which word was ambiguous, what did the literal follower do versus what we expected, what is a clearer version? Write the rewrite on the board. If still ambiguous, push: "Could a literal follower still pick the wrong meaning?"
  5. Repeat for slip #2 (4 min) and slip #3 (4 min). Same loop: live follow → debrief → board rewrite. New volunteer each time if one wants to step in.
  6. Sticky-note round (8 min). Each student writes one short ambiguous instruction from their own life on a sticky note. Seeds: "tidy your room", "be ready by 7", "do your homework after dinner". Collect them, read three at random, let the class disambiguate together; write the cleanest rewrite on the board next to each.
  7. Close (2 min). Point at the board. "Every rewrite started as something that sounded fine in someone's head. The follower made the hidden ambiguity visible. You can do this to your own instructions on purpose."

Debrief questions (use 2–3 in the closing minute or save for Lesson 2's warm-up).

  • Which slip caused the biggest gap between what we expected and what the volunteer did?
  • Which of the four ambiguity patterns from the lesson (vague nouns, deictic words, relative time, vague quantity) showed up most often?
  • Was any rewrite still ambiguous after we "fixed" it? What was still loose?

Why it requires the classroom. The embodied, public moment of literal interpretation is the whole point. A student doing this alone does not experience the small shock of seeing a familiar instruction misfire in front of them.

Activity B — "Algorithm relay (pair work)" 🟣 OPTIONAL

Goal. Students discover that the steps of an algorithm carry the goal; if the steps are clear, the goal can be inferred. Makes "what an algorithm is" concrete from the other side.

Group size. Pairs. (Odd numbers: one group of three; the third is timekeeper and second guesser.)

Time required. 20 min (Lesson 2, 60–80 min slot, or as a Lesson 3 warm-up).

Teacher preparation (before class).

  1. A board prompt with the rules of the relay so students can refer to it without asking.
  2. Optional: 3–4 fallback goals for pairs who cannot think of one (e.g. making instant noodles, sending a school absence message, charging a laptop, closing all the tabs in a browser).
  3. No props beyond paper and a pen per student.

Step-by-step facilitation.

  1. Explain the relay (2 min). Pair A writes a 6-step everyday algorithm without naming the goal. Pair B reads it and tries to guess the goal from the steps alone. Then swap.
  2. Round 1, Pair A writes (5 min). Pair A picks a goal silently, writes 6 numbered steps, and does not write the goal on the paper.
  3. Round 1, Pair B guesses (4 min). Pair B writes their best guess at the bottom, plus one sentence: "The step that gave it away was step , because ." They also note any step too vague to help.
  4. Reveal and discuss (2 min). Pair A reveals the goal. Which step gave it away, which was unhelpful, would a literal follower have reached the goal?
  5. Swap roles (5 min). Pair B writes, Pair A guesses.
  6. Whole-class debrief (2 min). Ask two or three pairs: "What kind of step was most useful for guessing the goal — a concrete action with a named object, or a general action without one?" Concrete steps ("pour boiling water into the cup") give the goal away; vague steps ("prepare it") do not. Connect to Lesson 3's topic of precision.

Debrief questions.

  • Did the guessing pair land on the exact goal, or on something close? What made the gap?
  • Was there a step where the guessing pair said "I cannot tell what this means"?
  • If the writing pair could change one step to make the goal clearer without naming it, which would they change?

Why it works as a classroom activity. The live reveal moment and the shared paper are the engine; pair work also forces every student to say something out loud.

How these activities connect to teacher-materials.md

  • Activity A (MANDATORY) appears in the Lesson 1 plan (5-min warm-up demo, single instruction) and in the Lesson 3 plan (full 25-min slot, 55–80 min). The Lesson 3 plan assumes Activity A has been prepared.
  • Activity B (OPTIONAL) is the default pair activity in the Lesson 2 plan (60–80 min). It can be swapped for a different pair activity if the teacher prefers.
