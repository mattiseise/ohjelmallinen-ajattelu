## What is computational thinking?

The habit of breaking a task into small, clear, ordered steps that a literal follower can carry out without guessing.

It is the thinking *behind* programming, not programming itself.

---

## A computer takes you literally

You ask your eight-year-old cousin: "Make me a sandwich."

She hands you a slice of bread.

She is not confused. You just did not give her enough to go on.

---

## "Make a sandwich" — what could go wrong?

- Which bread?
- How many slices?
- What goes inside?
- Cut in half or not?
- Is a single slice a sandwich?

A literal follower has to decide every one of these.

---

## Ambiguity is in the instruction, not the computer

The computer is **literal**. It does exactly what is written.

When something breaks, the question is not what is wrong with the machine.

It is "where was I unclear?"

---

## Algorithm — a name for what you already do

An algorithm is a clear sequence of literal steps that gets you from a known input to a known output.

A recipe is an algorithm. The route to school is an algorithm. Your morning routine is an algorithm.

---

## Parts of an algorithm

- **Goal** — what you are trying to end up with
- **Input** — what you start with
- **Steps** — the actions in order
- **Output** — what you actually get at the end

---

## Two algorithms, one goal

Route A: walk to Park Street, take bus 12, get off at Market, walk two blocks east.

Route B: walk fifteen minutes to the tram stop, take tram 4, get off at School Square.

Same goal. Both correct. Two valid algorithms.

---

## Precise vs. imprecise instructions

Imprecise: *"Save the file in the usual place."*

Precise: *"Save `report-may.docx` into the shared drive folder `Team/Reports/2026/May`."*

Length is not the point. **Number of guesses left to the follower** is the point.

---

## Hunting ambiguity — four patterns

- **Vague nouns** — *the file, the thing, the email*
- **Deictic words** — *here, there, it, him*
- **Relative time** — *later, soon, after lunch*
- **Vague quantities** — *a bit, some, a few*

When you spot any of these, ask: could a literal follower do this in more than one way?

---

## Testing as a literal robot

You wrote an instruction. You know what you meant.

When you re-read it, you read what you meant — not what you wrote.

The only fix: hand it to a real follower, or follow it yourself with no memory of what you meant.

---

## Edge cases (gentle intro)

*"Open the fridge and take out the milk."*

What if there is no milk? What if there are two cartons? What if one is past its date?

The instruction is fine for the common case. It does not say what to do otherwise.

## Core minimum vs. deepening

**Core minimum**
- Define the five core terms
- Write a short everyday algorithm
- Spot and fix one ambiguous word

**Deepening**
- Hand your instruction to a real follower
- Vocational angle: a help-desk instruction
- LLM sparring with a structured prompt
- Notice and describe one edge case
