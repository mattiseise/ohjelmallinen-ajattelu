# Week 1 — Speaking to a computer

## Week 1 theme

The whole course is built on one question: **How do I speak to a computer so that it understands what I want it to do?** Across three lessons you will see that a computer follows instructions exactly as written, and that the everyday skill of telling someone how to do something is already a form of computational thinking. Lesson 1 names the problem. Lesson 2 names the everyday tool that solves it — the algorithm. Lesson 3 stress-tests that tool by hunting ambiguity and rewriting.

## What you will be able to do by the end of the week

- State the central question of the course in your own words.
- Explain that a computer follows instructions literally, and that ambiguity lives in the instruction.
- Define *computational thinking*, *instruction*, *algorithm*, *ambiguity* and *precision* in plain English.
- Identify the goal, input, steps and output of a familiar activity.
- Write a short (5–10 step) everyday algorithm in plain English.
- Find one unclear word in an instruction and rewrite the instruction more precisely.

## Core minimum vs. deepening

**Core minimum — every student should leave Week 1 able to:**
- Define the five core terms above in plain English.
- Write one short everyday algorithm (for example a recipe for tea, or the route from home to school).
- Spot one ambiguous word in a given instruction and propose a clearer version.

**Deepening (optional, not required for the assessed task):**
- Give your instruction to a real follower and rewrite it after watching them get stuck.
- Apply the literal-follower lens to a vocational IT scene (a help-desk reply, a README, a chat-bot instruction).
- Use an LLM as a sparring partner against your own analysis, with a structured prompt.
- Notice and describe one edge case — an unusual situation where a good instruction breaks.

## How the three lessons fit together

The three lessons form one arc. **Lesson 1** sets the scene: a computer is a literal follower, so the quality of the instruction decides the outcome. **Lesson 2** names the artefact good instructions take — the algorithm — and asks you to write your first one. **Lesson 3** turns a critical eye on what you wrote: read it as a literal follower would, find the ambiguous words, rewrite. Problem, artefact, stress-test.
