## Slide 1 — Week 1 — Speaking to a computer

Welcome the class. Set the tone: this is not a coding course yet — it is a thinking course about how to give instructions that actually work. By the end of the week, students should be able to spot the moment a normal sentence becomes too sloppy to hand to a machine. Keep it light. Tell them the only prerequisite is a willingness to be picky about words. Ask the class: "When you tell your phone to do something and it does the wrong thing, whose fault is it?" Hold the answer. We come back to it. ~2 min.

## Slide 2 — The central question

Read the question out loud, slowly. This sentence is the spine of the whole week — every lesson loops back to it. Tell students you will ask it again on Friday. The aim of the week is not memorising terms; it is being able to answer this question in their own words. Ask the class: "What is the difference between speaking to a friend and speaking to a computer?" Take two or three answers without judging — write them on the board. We will revisit them at the end of the week and see how they have shifted. ~2 min.

## Slide 3 — What this week trains

Walk through the three lessons in one breath so the arc is visible: literal-follower, algorithm, precision. Emphasise that these are not three separate topics — they are three angles on the same shift in mindset. The shift: from everyday language (which leaves a lot to the listener) to instruction language (which leaves as little as possible). Ask: "Has anyone written a how-to message — for a friend, a sibling, a customer — and had it go wrong?" That story is exactly what we are unpacking. ~2 min.

## Slide 4 — What is computational thinking?

Make the distinction sharp: computational thinking is not programming. It is the thinking that has to happen before the typing. The phrase to land is "a literal follower can carry out without guessing." Underline "without guessing" — every guess the follower has to make is a place the instruction can break. Concrete example: a help-desk script. If the script says "check the user's settings", the new hire has to guess which settings. That guess is the bug. Ask: "What does the word literal mean to you?" ~3 min.

## Slide 5 — A computer takes you literally

Tell the cousin-and-sandwich story like a joke — pause for the laugh. The point: the cousin is not stupid; she is honest. She gave you exactly what you asked for. Computers are the same. This is the single most important reframe in the week. When a script breaks, the student's first instinct is "the computer is broken" — we are replacing that with "where was I unclear?" Ask the class: "What did the cousin do right?" Push for "she followed the words". ~3 min.

## Slide 6 — "Make a sandwich" — what could go wrong?

Read each bullet and pause. Let students see how many decisions are hidden inside three innocent words. The job here is not to find the right sandwich — it is to feel how many gaps the cousin (or the computer) has to fill in alone. Ask: "If I gave this instruction to ten people, how many different sandwiches would I get back?" Then: "Which of those would I call wrong?" The answer is none — they all followed the words. ~4 min.

## Slide 7 — Ambiguity is in the instruction, not the computer

Land the sentence: "where was I unclear?" Write it on the board. This is the reframe that unlocks the rest of the course. In IT work, this is the difference between blaming the machine and debugging your own request. Concrete example: a script fails because the path "C:\Users\Documents" does not exist on the new laptop — the script did exactly what it was told. Ask: "Whose problem is that?" The honest answer is the writer's. ~3 min.

## Slide 8 — Algorithm — a name for what you already do

De-mystify the word "algorithm". Students hear it and think of secret maths; in this course it just means an ordered sequence of clear steps. They already use them every morning. Tying the word to brushing teeth or catching the bus makes it concrete. Ask: "What is one algorithm you ran today before walking into this room?" Take two or three. Write them on the board in a numbered list — you have just modelled the format we will use all week. ~3 min.

## Slide 9 — Parts of an algorithm

These four words — goal, input, steps, output — are the scaffolding for every algorithm in this course. Make students write them down. The supplement deck walks an instant-noodle example through all four parts; reference it. Concrete IT example: backing up a folder. Goal = a recoverable copy. Input = the folder + a target drive. Steps = the actual copy actions. Output = the copy on the drive. Ask: "What happens if the input is missing?" Answer: the algorithm has nowhere to start. ~4 min.

## Slide 10 — Two algorithms, one goal

Two routes to school, both valid. This slide quietly previews Week 4 (comparing solutions), but do not introduce that word yet. The point right now is humility: there is rarely one right algorithm. Tell students this is good news — they will not be graded on finding the one true answer. They will be graded on whether their algorithm actually gets a literal follower to the goal. Ask: "Could a third route also be correct?" Yes — as long as it ends at the school. ~3 min.

## Slide 11 — Precise vs. imprecise instructions

Read both versions out loud — the imprecise one first, then the precise one. The phrase to underline is the last line: "number of guesses left to the follower". Length is irrelevant; what counts is whether the follower can act without checking back. Concrete: a colleague's "save the file in the usual place" forces you to interrupt them. The longer sentence saves them ten minutes of Slack messages. Ask: "Which version would you rather receive on a Friday afternoon?" ~3 min.

## Slide 12 — Hunting ambiguity — four patterns

These four are the diagnostic kit for the rest of the week. Read each pattern and give one quick example from class life: "the email" (vague noun), "send it to him" (deictic), "I'll fix it later" (relative time), "add a few users" (vague quantity). Tell students to learn to feel a small alarm bell when they hear any of these in their own writing. Ask: "Spot one of these in a message you have sent this week." Quick pairs. ~4 min.

## Slide 13 — Testing as a literal robot

This is the trickiest idea so far. When you read your own instruction, your brain quietly fills in everything you meant. You cannot test your own writing the way a literal follower would read it. The fix is honest: hand it to someone, or wait until the meaning has faded from your head. Concrete: students will pair up later and follow each other's instructions exactly — no rescuing each other. Ask: "Why is it so hard to find bugs in your own writing?" ~3 min.

## Slide 14 — Edge cases (gentle intro)

Introduce edge cases lightly — we go deeper in Week 5. The point now is just naming the idea: every instruction is fine for the case the writer was thinking about. The interesting question is what happens for the cases they were not thinking about. Concrete: the fridge-and-milk example, then a help-desk version — "ask the user to log in again". Fine if they have one account. What if they have three? What if they cannot remember which? Ask: "What is one edge case for 'open the fridge'?" ~4 min.

## Slide 15 — Week 1 vocabulary at a glance

Read each term aloud. Tell students the vocabulary sheet is in their folder; they do not need to memorise definitions tonight. They do need to recognise these words by Friday. The two highest-priority terms are "algorithm" and "literal interpretation" — those carry into Weeks 2 and 3. Ask: "Which of these words have you heard outside school?" That gauges where they are starting. ~2 min.

## Slide 16 — Core minimum vs. deepening

Be explicit about the two-track structure. Everybody does the core minimum. The deepening tasks are for students who want more bite — they are not extra credit, they are extra depth. Reassure students that "core minimum" is not a euphemism for "easy"; it is the floor for passing this week. Ask: "If you finish the core minimum early, which deepening task would you pick?" ~2 min.

## Slide 17 — Tasks this week

Walk through the four tasks. Task 1.1 is mandatory — the literal robot game, played in pairs. The others are deepening. Stress that their notes from this week feed into AT1 at the end of Week 2 — do not throw the paper away. Concrete: tell them AT1 is a real submission, not a quiz. Ask: "Which task scares you the most?" That answer often picks itself the right partner. ~3 min.

## Slide 18 — What comes next: Week 2 — decomposition

Tease the bridge. The literal-follower mindset stays — they do not unlearn anything. Next week we take a task that is too big for one flat list and break it into smaller algorithms. Concrete: "set up a new colleague's laptop by 10:00" is too big for one list, but each subtask is exactly the kind of algorithm we wrote this week. Ask: "What is one task in your week that is too big for one list?" ~2 min.

## Slide 19 — Recap — the central question, one more time

Close the loop. Read the question one more time. Ask the class to answer it now, in one sentence, in their own words. Take three or four answers. Compare against the answers from Slide 2. If the language has tightened — fewer "kind ofs", more "step by step" — you have landed Week 1. Send them off with the homework reminder and a deliberate beat of silence. ~3 min.
