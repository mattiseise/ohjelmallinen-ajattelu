## Slide 1 — Week 2 — Decomposing problems

Welcome back. Open with the line that anchors the week: last week you wrote one clean instruction; this week the instruction is too big for one list, so you learn to split it. Tell students the literal-follower mindset does not go anywhere — it still applies to every subtask they will draw today. The new skill is naming the parent task, listing its children, and putting the children in an order that respects what depends on what. Ask: "What is the biggest task on your week's to-do list right now?" Hold that answer — we may decompose it together later. ~2 min.

## Slide 2 — What we covered in Week 1

Walk the three bullets in one breath. The point is not nostalgia; it is to remind students that decomposition does not replace last week's mindset — it builds on top of it. Each subtask we name today still has to be a clear instruction that a literal follower can carry out. Ask: "Without looking, can someone give me Week 1 in one sentence?" If they say "a computer takes you literally", you are in good shape. ~2 min.

## Slide 3 — This week's central message

Read the quoted sentence slowly. Underline three words on the board: main task, subtasks, order. Those are the three things you will be looking for in every artefact this week. Tell the class you will return to this sentence on Friday, in their own words. The bridge from Week 1: precise instructions are still the unit, but now you stack and order them. Ask: "Which of those three — main task, subtasks, or order — sounds easiest? Which sounds hardest?" Their answer often previews where AT1 will trip them up. ~3 min.

## Slide 4 — What is decomposition?

Define the word out loud. The phrase to land is "splitting it into a main task with a goal and a small set of subtasks, each with its own sub-goal." Stress that decomposition is a planning move — the actual steps still exist; you have just zoomed out one level. Concrete: "set up a new laptop" is not a step. It is a parent. Underneath it sit "install the OS", "create the user account", "install office tools" — each its own algorithm. Ask: "What does sub-goal mean to you?" Push for "what is true after this subtask, but not yet after the whole thing". ~4 min.

## Slide 5 — Main task, subtasks, goal

These four words form the vocabulary scaffolding for the week. Make students write them down. Main task and goal travel together — the task is what you are doing, the goal is what is true at the end. Subtask and sub-goal mirror that one level down. Concrete IT example: main task = "share the lesson handout with the class"; one subtask = "find the document" with sub-goal "the file is open on my screen". Ask: "Why bother with sub-goals?" Answer: because they give you a checkpoint — you can stop and verify before moving on. ~4 min.

## Slide 6 — Everyday example — making breakfast for four

Walk the seven subtasks. The point is that "make breakfast for four by 9:30" is not a single instruction you can hand to anyone — but each bullet underneath it almost is. Notice that some subtasks must come before others (decide the menu before you cook), and some are independent (set the table can happen anytime). We are not naming dependencies yet — just feeling that they exist. Ask: "Which of these subtasks could you start right now without waiting for anything?" That question is the warm-up for the dependency slide. ~4 min.

## Slide 7 — IT example — creating a user account

Same shape, vocational context. Four subtasks, each one a small algorithm. Tell students that this is the exact size of decomposition AT1 will ask for — four to eight subtasks. Bigger than this and they have not split enough; smaller and they have not actually decomposed. Concrete: "send the confirmation" is a subtask, not a step — underneath it sit "open the email tool", "compose the message", "press send". We just chose not to draw that level. Ask: "Why stop at four subtasks here and not zoom in further?" ~4 min.

## Slide 8 — Why decompose at all?

Three honest reasons, all visible in their own school week: smaller pieces are easier to write, easier to split between people, and easier to check. Land the word "checkpoint" — students should hear it again at AT1. Concrete: when an IT team sets up a new classroom, no one person does everything; one person handles network, one handles devices, one handles software. They can only divide the work because someone decomposed the task first. Ask: "Have you ever split a group project? Who decided how to split it?" That choice is decomposition. ~3 min.

## Slide 9 — Some subtasks depend on others

Define dependency in their own words: one subtask cannot start until another finishes. The toast-and-eat example is deliberately silly so the idea sticks. Underline that a dependency is a connection in the output → input direction — the output of one subtask becomes the input of the next. Concrete IT example: "send the share link" needs the output of "set the permissions". Ask: "Name two subtasks in 'open a new bank account' where one depends on the other." ~4 min.

## Slide 10 — Some subtasks are independent

The counter-case. "Set the table" can happen any time relative to "prepare hot food" — the order is free. Be careful with the word "parallel" — in this course it means "could happen in either order or at the same time", nothing fancier. We are not doing concurrency. The honest test: if I swap the two subtasks, does the algorithm still work? If yes, they are independent. Ask: "In creating a user account, which subtasks could be done in either order?" ~3 min.

## Slide 11 — Putting subtasks in order — birthday party

Show the messy list first, then the cleaned-up order. Walk through why each dependency forces its position: you cannot send invitations without a guest list, and you cannot make a guest list without a date. The other subtasks — cake, snacks, decoration, music — float; pick any defensible order. The honest message: not every decision has one right answer, but every dependency does. Concrete classroom version: planning a study session — pick a time, pick a room, then invite people. Ask: "What is the first dependency in your week's biggest task?" ~5 min.

## Slide 12 — IT example — sharing a file

Six subtasks, two dependencies, one free branch. Read the bullets; mark the dependencies on the board. Notice "write the email body" is free of "copy the share link" — they can happen in either order. "Send the email" depends on both. This is the exact shape AT1 will ask for. Ask: "If we added 'attach the rubric' to this task, where would it go in the order?" Pick a volunteer; defend the answer. ~5 min.

## Slide 13 — Decomposing a real IT task — Lesson 6 preview

Tease Lesson 6: a real vocational task, decomposed live in class. Read the quoted task out loud. Tell students the artefact they produce in L6 is the body of AT1 — they leave that lesson with the submission almost done. The point to land: this is not a worksheet, it is something a working IT person actually does. Ask: "What would go wrong if the colleague's laptop was not ready on time?" That answer is why decomposition matters. ~3 min.

## Slide 14 — Two valid decompositions of one task

The honest message: there is not one right way to decompose. Two colleagues, two valid breakdowns. The criterion is not "which is correct" but "does each one let a literal follower work the subtasks?" This previews Week 4's "compare two valid solutions" move. Concrete: a Windows admin and a Mac admin would decompose "set up a new laptop" differently because their tools group things differently. Both right. Ask: "Could a student decompose differently from a teacher and still be right?" Yes. ~3 min.

## Slide 15 — Week 2 vocabulary at a glance

Read each term aloud. Priority terms for AT1: decomposition, main task, subtask, sub-goal, dependency. The others are useful but secondary. Vocabulary sheet is in their folder; they do not need to memorise definitions tonight — they do need to use these words correctly in their AT1 submission. Ask: "Which word would you struggle to define right now?" Take two answers and clean them up on the board. ~2 min.

## Slide 16 — Tasks this week

Two mandatory tasks (2.1, 2.2), three deepening. 2.1 is solo, 2.2 is in pairs — the partner re-tests the decomposition, which is how students discover gaps in their own work. Stress that both mandatory tasks feed directly into AT1; they are not throwaway practice. Concrete: tell them the partner's job in 2.2 is to be a literal follower, not a rescuer. Ask: "Which deepening task would you pick if you finish early?" ~3 min.

## Slide 17 — AT1 — the first assessed task

The first real submission. Walk the rubric line by line. Four to eight subtasks, sub-goals named, order with at least one dependency called out, plus a short reflection. No grade for "best decomposition" — the grade is for whether the decomposition holds together under a literal follower's eye. Concrete: their notes from this week are the raw material. Tell them not to start AT1 from scratch on Friday — it is already half-written in their notebook. Ask: "What is the reflection actually asking for?" Answer: what you changed and why. ~5 min.

## Slide 18 — What comes next: Week 3 — abstraction and flowcharts

Tease the bridge. Week 3 adds two things on top of decomposition: abstraction (hiding the detail that does not matter for the audience) and the flowchart (a picture of the process). The literal-follower mindset and the parent/subtask habit both stay. Concrete: this week's "set up a laptop" decomposition becomes next week's flowchart with one diamond and three rectangles. Ask: "What might be missing from a list that a picture would show clearly?" ~2 min.

## Slide 19 — Recap — the central message, one more time

Close the loop. Read the quoted sentence again. Ask students to say it back to you in their own words — main task, subtasks, order. Take three or four answers. If they cleanly name dependencies as well, you have over-landed the week and they are ready for AT1. Send them off with the AT1 reminder, the deadline, and a single beat of silence. ~3 min.
