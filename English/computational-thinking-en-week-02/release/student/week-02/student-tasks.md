# Week 2 — Student tasks

## Overview of the week's tasks

This week has **five tasks**. The two with the green dot (🟢 MANDATORY) are the **must-do minimum**. The three with the purple dot (🟣 DEEPENING) are optional extensions.

**AT1 lands at the end of Lesson 6.** It asks you to decompose one task (everyday or IT-related) into a main task with a goal, 4–8 subtasks with sub-goals, and an order with at least one dependency called out. The notes from these tasks are AT1's raw material — throw nothing away.

The computer is **literal**, not stupid. Every subtask must still be small enough for a literal follower to carry out without guessing.

---

## Task 2.1 — Decompose one task from your own week 🟢 MANDATORY

**Goal.** Take one real task from your week and split it into a main task, named subtasks with sub-goals, plus a short reflection.

### Steps

1. **Pick one real task from your own week.** Not a hypothetical. Something on your calendar between today and next Sunday — a project deadline, a sports practice you organise, a trip home, a birthday.
2. **Name the main task and its goal** in one short sentence each. The goal says when you are done.
3. **Write 4–6 subtasks.** Each one short sentence. For each, write its **sub-goal** in one sentence: *what is true once this subtask is done?* Sub-goals are commonly forgotten — do not skip them.
4. **Ask of each subtask: could a literal follower carry this out as written?** If not, add a one-line note: *"would need to be split further into …"*. You do not have to split it; just notice.
5. **3–5 sentence reflection.** Which subtask was hardest to name a sub-goal for? Was any much bigger? What surprised you?

### If you are doing this alone

No partner needed. For a sharper check, use the **24-hour gap**: write today, read tomorrow as if a colleague handed it to you, and cross out any sub-goal you cannot understand on a fresh read.

> 💡 **Tip:** If two subtasks share the same sub-goal, you probably wrote one twice. Merge them, or rewrite the sub-goals so each names a different "what is true once this is done".

### Keep your notes

Keep the main task, the goal, the subtasks with sub-goals, and the reflection. **AT1 at the end of Lesson 6** asks for a decomposition of an everyday or IT-related task — this is your warm-up artefact. If you later submit your everyday task, most of AT1 is already done.

## Task 2.2 — Decompose with a partner, then re-test 🟢 MANDATORY

**Goal.** Hand a written decomposition to a real partner, watch which subtasks they cannot follow, and rewrite. This is the must-do minimum for partnered practice.

### Steps

1. **Pick a small everyday task someone else could plausibly carry out.** Good choices: *make a sandwich for someone with a specific dietary need*; *organise a small surprise*; *plan a 2-day camping trip*; *move a shared bookshelf*.
2. **Write the decomposition.** 4–6 numbered subtasks, each with a one-sentence sub-goal.
3. **Hand it to a partner.** Brief them in one line: *"Read this once. Ask me at least three clarifying questions about whatever you cannot follow. I will stay quiet until you ask."* Then do not coach.
4. **Write down the clarifying questions, word for word**, and note which subtask each is about.
5. **Rewrite the decomposition** to fix the ambiguities. The new version can be longer.
6. **3–5 sentence reflection.** Which subtask was most ambiguous, and why? Was the ambiguity in the subtask itself, the sub-goal, or the link between them?

### If you are doing this alone

If you cannot find a partner, use one of these (do at least one):

- **24-hour gap.** Write today. Tomorrow, with no notes, read it as if a colleague handed it to you. Write down the three questions you would ask, then rewrite as in step 5.
- **Class chat.** Post with one line: *"Trying this for a class task — would anyone tell me which subtask they got stuck on?"* One reply is enough.

> 💡 **Tip:** Your partner's job is to be a literal reader, not a helpful one. If they "fill in the blanks", the test does not work. Ask them to take every sub-goal at face value and stop when they cannot.

### Keep your notes

Keep both versions (before and after), the clarifying questions, and your reflection. **AT1 lands at the end of Lesson 6**, and the partner-tested version is a strong candidate if your everyday task feels stronger than your IT one.

## Task 2.3 — Order and dependencies on a familiar task 🟣 DEEPENING

**Goal.** Take a decomposition you already have (Task 2.1 or 2.2) and add the second move of the week: identify which subtasks **depend** on which, and which are **free** in the order.

### Steps

1. **Pick one of your own decompositions** — or write a fresh one in 4–6 subtasks.
2. **For each subtask, ask:** which other subtask's output is this subtask's input? That is your dependency. Which could happen in either order? Those are free.
3. **Re-draw as a numbered ordered list.** Next to each subtask, write the dependency note in plain English: *"depends on subtask 2 (needs the bread already toasted)"* or *"no dependency"*.
4. **Mark at least one dependency with a short arrow note**, e.g. `subtask 4 ← subtask 2`.
5. **3–5 sentence reflection.** Which subtasks were free? Why does that matter? Did spotting dependencies make any subtask feel bigger than it looked?

### If you are doing this alone

No partner needed.

> 💡 **Tip:** If *every* subtask depends on the one before it, you have written a flat checklist rather than a decomposition. Check whether two middle subtasks could swap places.

### Keep your notes

Keep the ordered version with dependency notes. **AT1 at the end of Lesson 6 requires at least one named dependency**, so this directly feeds the artefact you submit.

## Task 2.4 — Vocational angle: decompose an IT task at your school 🟣 DEEPENING

**Goal.** Apply decomposition to a concrete IT task that exists in your school. Your school's specific software names, menu paths and rules force subtasks no generic example would catch.

### Steps

1. **Pick one concrete IT task that exists in your school.** Good choices: *set up a new student's email on a school laptop*; *reset a forgotten password from the help-desk side*; *share a folder with a class group*; *install school-licensed software on a classroom PC*; *connect a new printer*. You should be able to walk over and verify the button names.
2. **Name the main task and its goal**, using real product names and labels.
3. **Decompose into 5–8 subtasks with sub-goals.** Be specific. *"Open Outlook"* is too small; *"Configure Outlook with the new account"* is right.
4. **Name dependencies.** Mark at least two (subtask X depends on subtask Y because its input is Y's output) and at least one subtask whose order is free.
5. **Write a one-paragraph help-desk explanation** for a colleague who has done this kind of task before but not at your school. 5–8 sentences.
6. **Optional: show it to a classmate** who has never done this task. Ask them to predict what subtask 3 would look like, and note whether the prediction matched. The mismatch is the most valuable evidence here.

### If you are doing this alone

If you cannot find a classmate, use one of these:

- **Class chat.** Post with one line: *"Trying this for a class task — would anyone tell me which subtask they don't understand?"* One reply is enough.
- **Different device.** Walk through it on a different device — phone vs. laptop, classroom PC vs. home. The change of context exposes assumptions ("the menu is in the top-right") that only worked on the device in your head.
- **24-hour gap.** As a last resort, read it fresh tomorrow.

> 💡 **Tip:** A school-IT decomposition with no real menu items and no real dependencies is a generic decomposition with a school sticker on it. Specificity is the point.

### Keep your notes

Keep the main task, the goal, the 5–8 subtasks with sub-goals, the dependencies, the help-desk paragraph, and (if you ran it) the classmate's prediction. **This is the strongest candidate for AT1.** Ship a clean version and you have most of AT1 already — bring it to Lesson 6 and you will only need a short reflection on top.

## Task 2.5 — Sparring partner: challenge your own decomposition 🟣 DEEPENING

**Goal.** Use an LLM (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) as a sparring partner that *challenges* your decomposition, instead of writing one for you. The point is your decisions, not the LLM's text.

### Steps

1. **Pick one of your own decompositions** from Task 2.1, 2.2 or 2.4. Paste it into the LLM with the prompt below. Do not let the LLM write a new decomposition.

```
You are my sparring partner, not my teacher. I am a vocational ICT
student practising decomposition: I have broken a task into a main
task, subtasks with sub-goals, and an order with dependencies.

Below is my decomposition.

[paste your decomposition here]

Please challenge me. For each subtask:
- Could it be split further? If so, into what?
- Is the sub-goal really specific to that subtask, or is it the
  main task's goal in disguise?
- Is there a dependency I missed?

Do not give me the "right" answer. Ask me a question that helps
me see the gap myself.
```

2. **Read the LLM's response once. Do not accept it as truth.** Treat each challenge as a question to answer with your own thinking.
3. **Decide: accept, modify, or reject** each challenge. Write one short sentence saying *why*.
4. **Rewrite the decomposition** with your accepted and modified changes only.
5. **3–5 sentence reflection.** Which kind of challenge was most useful — *split further*, *sub-goal too generic*, or *missed dependency*?

**Do not paste the LLM's text into your final decomposition.** The LLM is the sparring partner; the decomposition is yours.

### If you are doing this alone

No human partner needed — the LLM is the sparring partner.

> 💡 **Tip:** If you accept more than 80% of the LLM's suggestions, you are not challenging it back hard enough. Ask: *would I have changed this if a human classmate had asked the same question?* If not, reject.

### Keep your notes

Keep your original decomposition, the LLM's challenges, your decisions with reasons, and the final rewritten version. **AT1 is submitted at the end of Lesson 6**, and a version that has survived an LLM sparring round is a strong AT1 candidate.
